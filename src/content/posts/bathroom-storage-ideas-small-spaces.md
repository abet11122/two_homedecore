Small bathrooms often have more usable wall and cabinet space than floor area. The right storage depends on what you need to keep in the room and which surfaces can hold it without making the fixtures harder to use.

## Start with a room and item map

List the products used each day, towels in use, spare supplies, cleaning products, and items that only occasionally need to be in the bathroom. Check the storage directions on medicines, cleaners, and anything affected by heat or humidity. Some items belong in a different room or in secured storage.

Then measure each proposed storage zone:

- **Floor:** Open the bathroom door, vanity drawers, shower door or screen, and toilet lid. Mark the space used by feet and elbows when using each fixture.
- **Vanity:** Record the clear inside width, depth, and height on each side of the plumbing, including hinges and the cabinet lip.
- **Wall:** Measure clear width and height, then test how far a shelf or cabinet could project without getting in the way.
- **Shower:** Note the wet surfaces, controls, drain, and cleaning access before choosing a caddy.

For a renovation, check the applicable local building and accessibility requirements. A fixed clearance from an article cannot account for every bathroom or household.

## Use the vanity for reachable groups

Under-sink storage works best when containers can be removed for cleaning and plumbing checks. A shallow bin may fit beside a trap where a wide stack of drawers will not. Make a paper template of the organizer's footprint and set it inside the cabinet. Open and close the doors, then lift the template out to test the retrieval path.

Keep daily toiletries at the front. Put occasional supplies behind them only if they remain visible or labeled. Avoid blocking shutoff valves. If the cabinet shows moisture damage, deal with that issue before filling it with products.

## Compare shelves and a cabinet above the toilet

A shelf can hold lightweight supplies such as spare rolls or hand towels. A closed cabinet hides packages and limits dust, but its doors need opening space. A freestanding frame needs room around the toilet base and water connections. Measure lid and flush access, standing headroom, and cleaning access with a paper or tape outline of the proposed unit.

Follow mounting and load instructions for shelves and cabinets. Check anti-tip instructions for a freestanding unit; renting does not make an unsecured tall unit safe to use. Avoid storing heavy, fragile, or spill-prone items overhead.

## Use doors and narrow wall areas carefully

An over-door organizer can hold light items if it does not stop the door closing or scrape the frame. Adhesive hooks and tension products depend on the wall finish, moisture, and the manufacturer's load instructions. Try a small number of hooks for towels or robes where the fabric can dry; a rail may be better if towels remain bunched and damp on hooks.

A narrow floor cabinet or rolling cart is useful only if it leaves the route and fixture access comfortable. Include handles, wheels, baseboards, and open doors in the footprint. A tall cabinet should be installed and anchored as directed by its maker.

## Choose shower storage for wet conditions

A tension corner caddy or properly mounted shelf can lift bottles off the floor. Check the product's load rating and whether it obstructs controls, a grab bar, or cleaning. Take it down periodically to clean behind it. Suction products can release, so do not place heavy or breakable items where a fall would be hazardous.

A recessed niche is a different project. Wall cavities are not uniform, and cutting into a shower wall affects waterproofing. Confirm the wall construction and services first, then plan the waterproofing and installation with someone qualified for that work.

## Example: decide with the actual obstruction

Suppose a vanity has 48 cm of clear internal width, but the plumbing and hinges split it into two usable side zones. Buying a 45 cm drawer based on overall width would fail. Sketch each side zone, test a paper footprint, and choose removable bins that leave access to the valves. The dimensions are illustrative; the decision comes from measuring the specific cabinet.

After using the layout for a week, notice which item still has to be moved to reach another. Adjust that zone first. More containers help only when they make retrieval and cleaning easier.
