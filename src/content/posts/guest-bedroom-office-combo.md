Count the days. The room is an office for something like 350 of them and a bedroom for perhaps 15. Almost every mistake in a dual-purpose room comes from designing it the other way round — a permanent double bed hogging the floor, a desk squeezed into a corner, and a room that works badly for most of the year so it can work perfectly for a fortnight.

Design for the 350. Then make the 15 easy.

## Choose the bed by how it behaves when nobody is in it

This is the whole decision, and everything else follows.

**A daybed.** The best all-rounder for most people. A real single mattress on a frame with a back and sides, so it reads as a sofa against the wall and needs no conversion at all. Add a trundle underneath if you occasionally need two beds. Comfortable to sit on, comfortable to sleep on, and nothing to fold away.

**A wall bed.** Buys the most floor by a distance — the bed is gone entirely during the day. The costs are real: it is the most expensive option, it must be fixed to a structural wall, and the mechanism needs clearance in front that you cannot use for anything permanent. Worth it in a genuinely small room you work in daily.

**A sofa bed.** Comfortable to sit on, usually the least comfortable to sleep on, and the conversion gets tedious. Choose one if the room doubles as a sitting room as well; otherwise a daybed beats it on both counts.

**A permanent double.** Only if the room is large enough that the desk is not compromised, or if guests stay often and for a long time. Honest about this: if the bed means the desk ends up as a 60 cm shelf in the corner, the room has failed at its main job.

**An air bed or folding mattress.** The cheapest answer, and perfectly respectable if guests come twice a year. Store it in the room so setting up is not an expedition to the loft.

## Put the desk where the light is right

Against a wall **perpendicular to the window**. Light falls across the desk from the side, rather than behind the screen — which makes you squint — or straight into your face on a video call.

Clearances that matter:

- **90 cm behind the chair**, so you can push back and stand.
- **Desk depth 50–60 cm** for a monitor; 45 cm is workable for a laptop only.
- Check what is behind you **in the camera frame**. A made daybed is a fine background; an unmade bed is not.

## Make the desk look like furniture

The thing that makes a dual-purpose room feel like an office with a bed in it is usually the desk, and specifically the hardware attached to it.

- **A wooden desk rather than a melamine office one.** It reads as a writing table when the laptop is away.
- **Cable management.** A trunking channel or an under-desk tray. Visible cables are the single loudest "this is an office" signal.
- **Somewhere to put the work away.** A closed cupboard, a drawer unit, or a basket for the laptop and papers. If the work can disappear in two minutes, the room converts in ten.
- **A chair that is not obviously a task chair,** if you can find one that still supports you properly. This is a genuine trade-off — do not wreck your back for the sake of a fortnight's aesthetics.

## What guests actually need

Less than people think, and it is not a wardrobe.

1. **Floor space for an open suitcase.** About 70 × 50 cm. This is the one most often forgotten, and its absence means a guest lives out of a bag on the bed.
2. **Two or three hangers**, on a hook or a short rail. Nobody needs a wardrobe for three nights.
3. **A bedside surface with a lamp** they can reach lying down. [Bedside table alternatives](/post/bedside-table-alternatives/) has the options for a tight gap.
4. **Two empty drawers or a cleared shelf.**
5. **Somewhere to charge a phone** within reach of the bed.

## The ten-minute switch

Write down what converting the room involves and then shorten the list.

The usual version: clear the desk into a box, swap the cushions for pillows, make up the bed, move the chair out, empty two drawers, put a towel on the end of the bed.

Keep the bedding **in the room** — under the daybed, in an ottoman, in the wall bed's cavity. A conversion that starts with a trip to the airing cupboard on another floor never takes ten minutes.

## Common mistakes

- **Designing for the guests.** They are here 15 days a year.
- **A permanent double that kills the desk.** The desk is the room's main job.
- **A desk facing a window.** Backlit screen, squinting, bad calls.
- **A sofa bed chosen for looks.** Lie on it in the shop.
- **Bedding stored elsewhere.** Keep it in the room.
- **No floor for a suitcase.** The most common oversight there is.
