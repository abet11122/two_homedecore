A rug that is too small is the most common furnishing mistake there is, and it is the one that makes a room look like a collection of objects rather than a space. The fix is not a bigger budget. It is measuring the furniture instead of the floor.

## Start with the seating, not the room

The instinct is to measure the room and buy a rug a bit smaller. That gives you a rug floating in the middle with every piece of furniture marooned around it.

Lay the furniture out first. Then ask one question: **does the rug reach the legs?**

- **All legs on.** Every piece of seating sits entirely on the rug. The most settled look, and the most expensive, because it needs a large rug.
- **Front legs on.** The front legs of the sofa and chairs sit on the rug, the back legs on the floor. This is the standard, it ties the seating together, and it is what most rooms should do.
- **All legs off.** Only the coffee table is on the rug. This works in a room with built-in seating or a window seat. In an ordinary room it reads as a rug that was too small.

Whichever you pick, apply it to *everything* in the seating group. A sofa on the rug with the armchair stranded off it is worse than either rule followed properly.

## Then check the margin

Once the rug catches the furniture, look at the other edge. You want a visible band of floor between the rug and the wall — **45–75 cm (18–30 in)** in a normal room. That margin is what makes the rug read as a deliberate island rather than a failed carpet.

In a small flat you will not get 45 cm, and that is fine. Twenty to thirty centimetres still does the job. What does not work is a rug running to the skirting board on every side.

If the room is awkward, the margin is also the thing to sacrifice first. Catching the furniture legs matters more.

## Common sizes, and what they actually hold

| Size (cm) | Size (ft) | Usually works for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 120 × 170 | 4 × 5'7" | A reading chair and a side table; a hallway landing spot |
| 160 × 230 | 5'3" × 7'7" | A small flat's sofa with front legs on, with a tight margin |
| 200 × 300 | 6'7" × 9'10" | The default living room: sofa plus two chairs, front legs on |
| 240 × 340 | 7'10" × 11'2" | A larger room, all legs on, or a dining table up to about 90 × 180 |
| 300 × 400 | 9'10" × 13'1" | Open-plan zoning, or a big room with all legs on |

If you are between two sizes, take the larger one. Nobody has ever stood in a room and thought the rug was slightly too generous.

## Dining rooms: the chair test

This one has an exact answer, because it is governed by a movement rather than a look.

Measure the table. Add **60 cm (24 in) on every side**. That is roughly how far a dining chair travels backwards when someone stands up. If the rug stops short of it, the back legs drop off the edge every single time somebody leaves the table — which is annoying, marks the rug, and eventually catches a toe.

A 90 × 180 cm table needs about 210 × 300 cm. A round table of 120 cm diameter needs about 240 cm across, round or square.

In a small flat where the dining table shares the living room, it is usually better to skip the rug under the table entirely than to put a too-small one there. [Zoning a studio](/post/studio-apartment-zoning/) covers how to handle a room doing two jobs at once.

## Bedrooms: three options

A bedroom rug is mostly about what your feet land on in the morning, so measure outward from the bed.

**One large rug, crosswise.** Set it under the lower two-thirds of the bed — not under the headboard or the bedside tables — so it extends 45–60 cm beyond both sides and the foot. A 200 × 300 cm suits a double or queen; a king usually wants 240 × 340.

**Two runners.** One down each side, roughly 80 × 200 cm. Much cheaper, works well in a narrow room, and gets the warmth exactly where you need it.

**One rug at the foot.** A small rug across the end of the bed. The weakest of the three, but it suits a room where the sides are tight against walls or wardrobes.

## Before you order

Tape it out. Mark the rug's footprint on the floor with masking tape and leave it for a day. Walk the room, open the doors, pull a chair out. It takes ten minutes and it is the only way to be certain.

Check the door clearance too, especially with a thick pile. An internal door usually has 10–15 mm under it, and a 20 mm rug will stop it.

## Rug mistakes worth avoiding

- **Buying to the room, not the furniture.** The legs rule beats the floor plan.
- **Stopping short in the dining room.** 60 cm on every side or don't bother.
- **Wall-to-wall.** Leave a margin, even a small one.
- **Going smaller to save money.** A small rug does not look thrifty; it looks like a mistake.
- **Forgetting the door swing.** Measure the clearance underneath.
- **Skipping the underlay.** It stops slipping, and it roughly doubles how long the rug lasts.
