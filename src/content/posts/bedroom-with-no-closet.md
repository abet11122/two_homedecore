The mistake is to start by shopping for furniture. A bedroom with no closet is a measuring problem first: you need to know how much of your clothing hangs, how much folds, and how much of it you realistically wear, before any of the options below can be judged.

## Measure the clothes, not the room

Take everything out and sort it into two piles: **hangs** and **folds**.

For the hanging pile, allow **2.5 cm of rail per shirt, blouse or light dress** and **4–5 cm per coat, jacket or heavy item**. Count them and add it up. Most people land somewhere between 90 and 150 cm of rail — considerably less than a full double wardrobe provides, which is useful to know before you buy one.

For the folded pile, count the stacks rather than the garments. A standard drawer takes two to three stacks side by side.

Then one more number: **55–60 cm**. That is the depth a hanging rail needs from the wall, because it is roughly how wide a shoulder sits on a hanger. Shallower than that and clothes hang skewed, catch on the front edge and crease.

If 55 cm is impossible, you need a **front-facing rail** — one that runs out from the wall so garments hang parallel to it, like a shop display. These work in 35–40 cm but hold far less.

## The options, by what suits them

**A freestanding wardrobe.** The obvious answer, and the right one if you have the floor area and rent. Measure the doors' swing as well as the carcass — a hinged double door needs around 50 cm of clear floor in front, which a bed often occupies. Sliding doors remove that problem and cost roughly a third of the internal width in overlap.

**An alcove with a rail and a curtain.** Almost always the best value in a room that has a chimney breast. Two brackets, a timber rail, a curtain track across the front. It uses space that is otherwise dead, costs a fraction of a wardrobe, and in a rental leaves four small holes. Measure the alcove depth first — many are 50–60 cm, which is exactly right.

**A chest of drawers plus a short rail.** Good when your folded pile is bigger than your hanging one, which is true for more people than expect it. The drawers do the heavy lifting; a 90 cm rail handles the few things that must hang.

**An open clothes rail.** Cheap, flexible, and entirely dependent on how disciplined you are, because everything on it is on permanent display. It also exposes clothes to dust and sunlight. If you like the idea but not the visual noise, put it in an alcove and curtain it.

**A storage bed.** Drawers under the base, or a gas-lift ottoman frame. Excellent for bedding, out-of-season clothes and things you reach for rarely. Not useful for daily clothes — nobody wants to lift a mattress before work. The [small bedroom storage guide](/post/small-bedroom-storage-ideas/) has the clearances for both types.

**Over-door hooks and the back of the door.** Marginal gains, but free, and the right place for dressing gowns, tomorrow's outfit and bags.

## Working out which

| If your clothes are... | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| Mostly hanging, and you have an alcove | Rail and curtain in the alcove |
| Mostly hanging, no alcove | Freestanding wardrobe, sliding doors if the bed is close |
| Mostly folded | Chest of drawers plus a 90 cm rail |
| Split, and the room is tiny | Storage bed for the bulk, short rail for the rest |
| Split, and you rent short-term | Freestanding rail and fabric drawers, nothing fixed |

## If you are fixing anything to the wall

A rail carrying a full load of winter coats is heavier than people assume — easily 20–30 kg across a 120 cm span. That is a real load, not a picture hook.

Find out what the wall is made of and use a fixing rated well above the weight. A rail into plasterboard alone, on the wrong anchor, comes down with a section of wall attached. The [floating shelf checklist](/post/diy-floating-shelves/) covers how to identify the wall type and what each one takes; the same checks apply to a rail.

In a rental, photograph every wall before you start and check the lease wording on fixings.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Buying the furniture first.** Measure the clothes. The answer is usually smaller than you think.
- **A rail less than 55 cm deep.** Clothes hang skewed and crease.
- **Forgetting the door swing.** A hinged wardrobe needs 50 cm of clear floor.
- **An open rail you will not keep tidy.** Be honest about this one.
- **Daily clothes under the bed.** It works for bedding and out-of-season things only.
- **The wrong wall fixing.** Coats are heavy. Check the wall first.
