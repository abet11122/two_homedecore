Two decisions matter in a small bathroom, and the colour chart is the second of them. The first is the finish — because a bathroom is the most hostile room in the house for paint, and the wrong product fails within a year regardless of how good the shade looked.

## Finish first

Use a paint **formulated for bathrooms or kitchens**. These are made to cope with moisture and to be wiped without burnishing. The tin will say so.

Ordinary matt emulsion is the common mistake. It absorbs moisture, marks permanently when you wipe a splash off it, and in a room with poor ventilation gives mould somewhere to take hold.

Sheen, from most to least suitable:

| Finish | Bathroom verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Dedicated bathroom paint | Best — formulated for humidity, wipeable |
| Eggshell or satin | Good — moisture-resistant, reflects light |
| Gloss | Durable, but shows every flaw in the wall |
| Matt emulsion | Avoid, whatever the colour |

Ventilation does more for a bathroom's walls than any paint. If the extractor is weak or there isn't one, that is the actual problem.

## Then reflectance, not colour

Paint brands publish a **light reflectance value** — LRV, from 0 for black to 100 for a theoretical pure white. It predicts how bright a colour will read far better than its name does.

**Above 70 is a bright wall.** Below 40 and the wall is absorbing most of what reaches it.

The instinct is to reach for the brightest white on the chart, and it is usually the wrong move. Most bathrooms are lit by artificial light for much of their use, and a cool brilliant white under electric light reads grey and clinical. A **warm off-white at LRV 78 will almost always feel brighter and more comfortable than a cool white at LRV 88.**

## The case for going dark

A windowless bathroom is where pale paint most often disappoints. White needs daylight to reflect, and with none available the room reads grey and flat no matter how high the LRV.

A small windowless bathroom painted a deep colour — forest green, ink blue, oxblood — and lit properly stops being a failed bright room and becomes a deliberate one. The darkness becomes the point.

This only works if the lighting is right, which in a bathroom means light at the mirror rather than overhead. [Bathroom lighting ideas](/post/bathroom-lighting-ideas/) covers that, and it is the prerequisite rather than an optional extra.

## Testing, properly

Bathrooms are small, enclosed and mostly artificially lit, which makes colour behave differently than on a chart.

1. **Paint a large sample** — A4 at minimum, ideally bigger — rather than relying on a chip.
2. **Put it on the darkest wall**, not the brightest.
3. **Look at it in the artificial light you actually use**, after dark as well as during the day.
4. **Hold it against the tile, the grout and the sanitaryware.** White sanitaryware is rarely the same white as paint, and a near-match looks like a mistake while a clear contrast looks intentional.
5. **Leave it a couple of days.**

That last point about near-matches is the one that catches people: a wall colour one step off the bath's white reads as a failed attempt rather than a choice.

## Working around the tile you have

Most small bathrooms come with tile that is not going anywhere.

- **Pick up an undertone from the tile** rather than fighting it. Grey tile wants a cool-leaning wall; cream tile wants a warm one.
- **Contrast clearly or match exactly.** The uncomfortable middle is what looks wrong.
- **Paint the grout line colour you cannot change into the scheme** by choosing a wall tone that makes it look deliberate.
- **Painting the tile itself** is possible with a tile primer and a suitable paint, though it is less durable than tile and needs real care in a shower enclosure. Preparation is the whole job, as in [how to update kitchen cabinets cheaply](/post/how-to-update-kitchen-cabinets-cheap/).

## If you rent

Check the lease before repainting; many require the original colour on exit. Where you cannot paint, the reflectance argument still applies to everything else: a large mirror, pale towels, a light shower curtain and a well-lit mirror will lift a dim bathroom without a brush. ## Where it goes wrong

- **Matt emulsion.** It fails in steam, whatever the colour.
- **Choosing by name rather than LRV.** The number predicts brightness.
- **Cool brilliant white under electric light.** It goes grey.
- **Testing on the brightest wall in daylight.** Test the worst case.
- **A wall colour almost matching the bath.** Contrast or match; not between.
- **Painting before fixing the ventilation.** That is the real cause of most bathroom wall problems.
