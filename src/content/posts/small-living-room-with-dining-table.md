A table in a living room fails for one reason more than any other: nobody measured the space behind the chairs. The table fits, the chairs fit tucked in, and then the first person to stand up discovers they cannot.

Start there, because it governs everything else.

## The number that decides it

**60 cm behind each chair that actually gets used**, measured from the table edge. That is the distance a dining chair travels when someone pushes back to stand.

So the floor a table needs is its own depth plus 60 cm on every side people sit. A 90 cm wide table with chairs on both long sides needs **about 210 cm of width** before anything else is in the room.

Where a side is against a wall and nobody sits there, you get that side back. A table pushed to a wall with seating on three sides is a common and sensible compromise in a small room.

The main route through the room still needs **75–90 cm**, and that route cannot be the same floor as the chair clearance — the two overlap only if nobody is sitting down, which is precisely when you need to walk past. ## Shape beats size

**Round.** Fits where a rectangle cannot, because the corners are gone and chairs tuck closer to the edge. A 90–100 cm round table seats four comfortably. It also removes the sharp corners from a walkway, which matters in a tight room.

**Drop-leaf.** One or both leaves down for everyday use against a wall, up when needed. The best answer where the table is used fully once a week and as a surface the rest of the time.

**Extending.** Same logic, more capacity, more weight and cost.

**Narrow rectangular.** A 70–75 cm depth rather than the usual 90 saves real floor and still seats people opposite each other.

**Square.** Fits a square-ish corner well, seats four, wastes less than a rectangle in a small room.

A pedestal base is worth looking for whichever shape you choose — no legs at the corners means chairs tuck in further and nobody barks a shin.

## Where each thing goes

**Table nearest the kitchen.** Carrying food is the daily activity; make it short.

**Seating furthest from the kitchen.** The two zones separate naturally, and the noise does too.

**The window** goes to whichever benefits more. Usually the table, since the sofa faces into the room anyway and a meal by a window is better than a sofa by one.

## Making it read as two zones

A single open room with a table at one end and a sofa at the other reads as a waiting area unless you mark the boundary. Three signals do it without a divider:

**Two rugs, not one.** One rug under the seating group, another under the table — sized so the chairs stay on it when pulled out, which means about 60 cm beyond the table on every used side. One large rug spanning both zones makes the room ambiguous. [What size rug do you need](/post/what-size-rug-do-i-need/) has the dining measurements.

**Separate lighting.** A pendant over the table, lamps in the seating area. This is the strongest signal of all — a pendant says *dining room* more clearly than any furniture arrangement.

**The back of the sofa as the line.** Face the sofa away from the table. A narrow console behind it reinforces the boundary and adds a surface for lamps.

[Zoning a studio](/post/studio-apartment-zoning/) takes the same approach further for a room doing three jobs.

## Chairs that earn their space

- **Armless.** Arms add width and stop chairs tucking under.
- **Light enough to lift**, so the table can be moved against a wall.
- **Stackable or folding** for the two extra you need occasionally.
- **A bench on one side**, which tucks fully under and takes two or three people in less width than chairs.

A bench against the wall side is the single most space-efficient seating decision available here.

## When the table is also a desk

Common in a small flat, and workable with one condition: the work needs somewhere to go. A closed box, a drawer unit, a basket — something that everything sweeps into before dinner.

Without that, the table stops being usable for eating within a fortnight. [Guest bedroom and office combo](/post/guest-bedroom-office-combo/) covers the same discipline applied to a room with a bed in it.

## Where it goes wrong

- **No clearance behind the chairs.** 60 cm, or people cannot stand up.
- **A rectangular table where a round one would fit.** Shape before size.
- **One rug across both zones.** Two rugs, two rooms.
- **No pendant.** It is the clearest zoning signal there is.
- **Chairs with arms.** They add width and will not tuck.
- **The table far from the kitchen.** You carry food every day.
