A small entryway works when people can open the door, step in, put down what they're carrying and leave again without stepping around anything. Measure the route with the door fully open, keep slim storage on one wall, and give keys, mail and wet shoes separate homes so none of them turns into a pile.

That's true whether your "entryway" is a proper hall or a 60 cm strip of wall beside the front door of a studio. The plan below works for both.

## 1. Measure the Route First

Open the front door all the way and mark where its edge sweeps across the floor. Then measure:

- **Clear walking width** from wall to wall, or wall to the nearest furniture.
- **Door swing** — the arc the front door and any nearby room or cupboard doors need.
- **Switches, thermostats, radiators and sockets** that storage must not cover.
- **Skirting board depth** — it pushes wall-mounted furniture out by 1–2 cm.

A comfortable walking width is about 90 cm (36 in). Many halls are 100–110 cm wide, so that leaves only 10–20 cm for storage on one side. That's why slim pieces matter.

**Example:** a hall is 105 cm wide. A 24 cm deep shoe cabinet leaves 81 cm — a little tight, but workable if nothing else projects. A 40 cm bench on the same wall would leave 65 cm, which feels cramped with a bag on your shoulder.

Before you buy, tape the furniture outline on the floor and walk through carrying shopping bags.

## 2. Count What Actually Comes Through the Door

For one normal week, notice what lands in the entry: coats, bags, shoes, keys, mail, umbrellas, dog leads, parcels. Then set limits:

- **One or two hooks per person** for the coat and bag they use now.
- **A fixed number of shoe spaces** — usually one or two pairs per person. Everything else lives in a wardrobe.
- **In-season only.** Winter coats move to a bedroom wardrobe in spring; sandals go away in autumn.

An entry overflows when it stores everything instead of handling what comes and goes each day.

## 3. Set Up Three Drop Zones

**Keys and small essentials.** One spot within arm's reach of the door: a key hook, a small wall shelf, or a dish on a narrow console. Pick one place and use it every time. A wall shelf 10–15 cm deep can also hold sunglasses and earbuds.

**Wet and dirty shoes.** A washable boot tray (often 35 × 75 cm) or a rubber-backed mat that lifts out for cleaning. Check that its edge doesn't catch the door. Let shoes dry here before they go into closed storage.

**Mail and paper.** A single wall file or slim letter tray. Empty it every few days into recycling, "to do" and "keep". The entry is a handoff point, not a filing cabinet.

## 4. Choose Hooks at the Right Heights

| Use | Mounting height | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult coats | 150–170 cm | Leave about 20–25 cm between hooks so coats don't bunch |
| Bags and children's coats | 90–110 cm | Doubles as a bag rail for adults |
| Dog leads and keys | 120–140 cm | Easy to grab on the way out |

A hook rail screwed into studs or with proper wall anchors is much stronger than individual adhesive hooks. Adhesive hooks suit light items only — a winter coat with a full pocket can weigh 2 kg, more than many adhesive hooks are rated for.

## 5. Pick Shoe Storage for Your Footprint

| Option | Typical depth | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tip-out shoe cabinet | 17–25 cm | Narrow halls; hides shoes | Must be wall-anchored; tall boots won't fit |
| Two-tier open rack | 25–30 cm | Fast drop-off; airflow for damp shoes | Visual clutter |
| Wall-mounted shoe shelves | 15–20 cm | Keeping the floor clear | Needs solid wall fixing |
| Bench with shoe shelf | 30–40 cm | Wider entries; somewhere to sit | Takes the most space |
| Under-bench baskets | 25–35 cm | Gloves, hats, bags | Must pull out without blocking the door |

Tip-out cabinets are the most space-efficient option for a narrow hall. Always fit the anti-tip bracket — a top-heavy cabinet with open drawers can fall forward.

## 6. Add a Bench Only If Someone Sits on It

A bench is only worth its depth if people actually sit to put on shoes — children, older family members, anyone with laced boots. If nobody sits, a slim cabinet gives you more storage in less space. If you do want somewhere to perch, a wall-mounted fold-down seat or a small stool that slides under a console takes very little room.

## 7. Use Vertical Space Above the Door Line

A shelf above a hook rail (at about 180–190 cm) holds hats, gloves and scarves in two or three baskets. Label them by person or category so they don't become one tangled bin. Keep anything heavy lower down.

## 8. Make It Pleasant Last

A mirror, a runner or a small lamp makes the entry feel welcoming — but add them after the route and drop zones work. A mirror opposite or beside the door bounces light into a dark hall and gives you a last check before you leave. ## What Clutters an Entry
- **Buying a bench for a hall that's too narrow.** Measure the remaining width first.
- **Storing every pair of shoes by the door.** Set a limit per person.
- **Skipping the anti-tip bracket.** Slim cabinets are top-heavy by design.
- **Adhesive hooks for heavy coats.** Screw a rail into the wall instead.
- **No home for mail.** It ends up on every flat surface in the flat.

## Review After a Week

Notice what still spills over. Shoes outside their spaces mean too many pairs or too little storage. A full mail file means it isn't being emptied. A hook nobody uses is in the wrong place. Fix that one zone first.
