The look is genuinely good: a long unbroken wall, the window uninterrupted, a room that breathes. The reason more kitchens do not do it is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is usually skipped until the cabinets are already in a skip.

## Count what the uppers are holding

Before anything else, empty the wall cabinets onto the table and sort what comes out into three piles:

- **Daily** — plates, bowls, mugs, glasses, the three pans you actually use.
- **Weekly** — serving dishes, the mixing bowl, the big sieve.
- **Rarely** — the roasting tin at Christmas, the second set of plates, the bread maker.

Then measure the shelf length you just emptied. In a typical kitchen, wall units hold somewhere around **a third of the total storage**, and most of the daily items are in them because they are at the most convenient height in the room.

That is the deficit you have to cover. If you cannot say where every item in the *daily* pile is going to live, removing the cabinets will make the kitchen worse to use, however much better it photographs.

## Where the storage goes instead

**Deeper drawers in the base units.** The most effective substitute. A deep pan drawer holds more than the cabinet above it did and is far easier to use, because you look down into it rather than reaching up and feeling around. If you are doing work anyway, this is where the budget belongs.

**A tall unit or larder cupboard.** One full-height cupboard holds a startling amount and keeps it out of sight. This is usually what makes a no-uppers kitchen actually function. In a rental, a freestanding version does the same job — [no pantry storage ideas](/post/no-pantry-storage-ideas/) covers choosing one.

**One long shelf.** The compromise, and often the right answer: a single shelf at around 50–55 cm above the worktop keeps the daily plates and mugs within reach while leaving most of the wall open. You lose the closed storage, not the convenience.

**A rail under the shelf or along the splashback.** Utensils, mugs on hooks, a knife strip. Takes the high-frequency small items off the worktop.

**Elsewhere in the room.** A dresser, a trolley, a shelf unit on the opposite wall. The storage does not have to be in the cooking zone if what is in it is not used while cooking.

## What you are signing up for

**Everything is on display, permanently.** There is no door to close on a bad day. A kitchen with no uppers needs a smaller, more coordinated set of things out, and it needs them kept that way. Be honest about whether that is you — [open shelving in the kitchen](/post/kitchen-open-shelving-styling/) goes into what that discipline actually involves.

**Dust and grease.** Open shelves near a hob collect an airborne film that closed cabinets do not. Items used daily get washed by use. Items used twice a year get washed before use, whether you want to or not.

**Reduced sound absorption.** Fewer soft-closing doors and fewer filled boxes on the wall make a hard room harder. Noticeable in a small kitchen.

**It is not cheaper.** Replacing wall units with a good tall cupboard and deep drawers usually costs more than leaving them alone.

## Who it suits

| Works well | Works badly |
| --- | --- |
| A kitchen with a tall larder unit or a pantry | A galley kitchen with no other wall to use |
| Someone who owns a modest, coordinated set of crockery | A household with a lot of mismatched kit |
| A room with a good window on that wall | A kitchen already short of storage |
| Cooks who tidy as they go | Anyone who needs a door to shut on the mess |
| An owned home, where drawers can be changed | A rental, where base units cannot be altered |

## What to put on the wall instead

The point of removing the cabinets is the wall, so it is worth doing something with it.

- **Tile the full height.** A continuous tiled or panelled wall is what makes the look read as deliberate rather than unfinished.
- **A deeper windowsill or a wider window reveal**, if the cabinets were crowding the window.
- **One picture or a small shelf with two objects.** Restraint is the whole point.
- **Nothing at all.** A plain painted wall above a worktop, well lit, is a perfectly good answer.

Under-cabinet lighting disappears with the cabinets, so plan a replacement: a wall light, a track, or a lit shelf. Otherwise the worktop ends up in the shadow of whoever is standing at it.

## If you are renting

You will not be removing anything, but the same thinking applies in reverse: if your rented kitchen already has no wall cabinets, the deficit is real and it is yours to solve. A freestanding tall cupboard, a trolley and a shelf unit will cover most of it, and all three leave with you. [Rental kitchen makeover](/post/rental-kitchen-makeover/) has the rest of the reversible options.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Removing before counting.** Measure the shelf length you are losing.
- **No tall cupboard anywhere.** This is what makes or breaks the whole idea.
- **Forgetting the under-cabinet lighting.** It goes with the cabinets.
- **Keeping the same amount of stuff.** The edit is permanent, not a one-off tidy.
- **Open shelves directly beside a hob.** Grease, every day.
- **Assuming it saves money.** Good drawers and a larder cost more than the units did.

If the storage maths does not work, the answer is usually better organisation rather than more cupboards — [small kitchen organization ideas](/post/small-kitchen-organization-ideas/) finds the space most kitchens are wasting.
