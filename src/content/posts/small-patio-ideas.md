A courtyard or a small paved yard is the outdoor space most often wasted, because it gets treated as leftover ground rather than as a room with no ceiling. The approach that works is the same one that works indoors: decide what it is for, then furnish it for that.

## Pick one function

A few square metres cannot be a dining room, a lounge and a garden. Choosing is what makes a small patio work.

**Eating.** A bistro table and two chairs, or a small square table for four if the space genuinely takes it. Needs about 90 cm of clear space behind each chair to push back and stand.

**Sitting.** A bench or two lounge chairs and a low side table. Takes less room, gets used more often in practice, and suits a space you pass through rather than linger in.

**Growing.** Planting as the main event, with somewhere to perch while you deal with it.

Most small patios should pick one and allow the second as an occasional guest — a folding chair brought out, a tray carried from the kitchen.

## Deal with the floor

Tired concrete is what makes most small patios feel like a service yard, and it is the quickest thing to change.

**Interlocking deck tiles.** Clip together over flat concrete, no fixing, and lift out when you leave — which makes them the obvious renter's answer. Two cautions: water still needs somewhere to drain, and tiles want lifting occasionally so damp and leaves do not sit underneath.

**An outdoor rug.** Defines a seating zone the same way a rug does indoors, and takes the edge off hard paving. Choose one made for outdoors and lift it when heavy rain is forecast, or the surface underneath stays wet.

**Gravel in the awkward strip.** The bit along a fence that nothing else solves.

**Paint.** Masonry or concrete paint on paving can work surprisingly well, with the usual caveats about preparation and slip resistance. Check the product is rated for foot traffic outside.

## Build privacy at the right height

Ground-floor overlooking is different from a balcony's. You are usually dealing with a neighbour's window, a path, or a shared boundary — and the fix is to break the sight line at **seated height**, not to build a wall.

- **Tall planters along the overlooked edge.** Grasses, bamboo in a container, or a small multi-stem tree. Flexible, movable, and nothing to get permission for.
- **A slatted screen or trellis.** Permeable, so it does not create a wind tunnel, and it takes climbers.
- **Climbers on wires.** Slower, and the best long-term answer. Jasmine or clematis on a sunny wall.

Check boundary rules and any height restrictions before fixing anything permanent, particularly on a shared fence — the fence may not be yours to attach to.

## Go vertical with the planting

Floor space is the thing you do not have, so the planting goes up the walls.

Wall-mounted pots, a vertical planter, a shelf of herbs by the kitchen door, hanging baskets on sturdy brackets. The ground stays clear for people, and the greenery still reads as a garden.

The weight and watering notes in [balcony container garden ideas](/post/balcony-container-garden-ideas/) apply here too — large containers are far more forgiving than small ones, and anything wall-mounted needs a fixing rated for the wet weight.

## Light it, or lose half the year

A patio without light gets used on summer afternoons and at no other time. Lighting is what turns it into a space you step out into on an evening in March.

**Several low sources, not one bright one.** Solar string lights along a boundary or a fence, two or three lanterns, a portable rechargeable light for the table. Warm, low and multiple reads as a room; a single security floodlight reads as a car park.

**Check what is rated for outdoors.** Anything mains-powered outside needs to be suitable for it and installed properly — that is an electrician's job, not a weekend one.

Then something for warmth — a blanket basket by the door does more for how often the space gets used than any heater.

## Make it feel bigger

The same moves that work in a small room work here:

- **One larger piece** of furniture rather than several small ones.
- **Planting at the edges**, floor kept clear.
- **Lay flooring in the longest direction** to stretch the space.
- **A tight palette** — two or three materials, not six.
- **One focal point**, whether that is a tree, a water bowl or a single good chair.

[How to arrange furniture in a small room](/post/how-to-arrange-furniture-in-a-small-room/) covers the reasoning; almost all of it transfers outside.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Furniture for three different activities.** Pick one.
- **A dining set that leaves no room to pull a chair out.** 90 cm behind each.
- **Deck tiles over an uneven or badly draining surface.** Check the base first.
- **Building privacy to full height.** Seated eye level is the sight line.
- **Fixing to a fence that is not yours.** Check first.
- **No lighting.** It halves the months you use it.
