Four routes, in ascending order of cost and effort. Most kitchens only need the first one.

| Option | Rough cost | Effort | Reversible |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New handles | Low | An hour | Yes |
| Paint | Low to moderate | 4–7 days | No |
| Adhesive film | Low | A weekend | Sometimes |
| New doors on existing carcasses | Moderate to high | A day, plus ordering | No |

## Handles first

Do this before considering anything else. It is an hour's work, it is completely reversible, and on a plain kitchen it changes the character of the room more per pound than any other option here.

**Measure the hole spacing before buying.** Handles come in fixed centres — most commonly **96, 128 and 160 mm**. Measure between the centres of two existing screw holes on a door. Buy a different spacing and you are drilling new holes, which rules the change out entirely in a rental and leaves you filling holes in an owned kitchen.

If the spacing is awkward or inconsistent across the kitchen, **knobs** take a single screw and fit anything.

While the doors are off for anything else, this is also the moment to adjust the hinges so the doors line up. A row of doors sitting square is worth more than people realise, and it costs nothing but a screwdriver.

## Painting: the preparation is the job

Painted cabinets either last for years or start chipping at the edges within months, and the difference is almost never the paint. It is what happened before the paint.

### 1. Take the doors off and label them

Number each door and its position with masking tape inside the carcass. Bag the hinges and screws by door. Painting doors flat on trestles gives a far better finish than painting them hanging, and it keeps drips off the fronts.

### 2. Degrease properly

Kitchen doors carry an invisible film of cooking grease, heaviest near the hob. Paint will not bond through it. Use a proper degreaser, work over every surface including edges and the backs, and rinse. Then do it again around the hob.

This is the step most often skipped and the most common single cause of failure.

### 3. Sand to a dull finish

You are not removing the existing finish, just keying it so the primer has something to grip. Fine abrasive, worked evenly until the shine is gone. Vacuum, then wipe with a tack cloth or a barely damp lint-free cloth.

### 4. Prime for the surface you have

- **Solid wood or already-painted wood:** a standard wood primer.
- **Laminate, melamine or thermofoil:** an **adhesion primer** rated for non-porous surfaces. This is not optional, and it is why laminate jobs fail.
- **Anything with knots or a strong wood stain:** a stain-blocking primer, or the colour will bleed through.

Read the tin for the surfaces it covers rather than assuming.

### 5. Two or three thin topcoats

Use a paint made for cabinets, trim or furniture — these cure harder than wall emulsion, which stays soft and marks. Thin coats, fully dry between each, following the manufacturer's recoat time rather than your own impatience. Thin and three beats thick and one every time.

A small foam roller on the flats and a good brush in the detail gives a clean finish without spray equipment.

### 6. Wait before you rehang

Paint is touch-dry long before it is hard. Most cabinet paints continue curing for days or weeks. Rehang when the instructions say, handle the doors gently at first, and expect full durability later than you would like.

**Total:** four to seven days for an average kitchen, of which perhaps a day and a half is work.

## Adhesive film

Vinyl film over the doors. Cheaper and faster than paint, and it can look convincing in a matt or woodgrain finish.

Where it works: flat slab doors, in a cool part of the kitchen, on clean dry surfaces.

Where it does not: shaker doors with a moulded profile, anywhere near the hob, and anywhere already peeling or damaged.

The same cautions as in [rental kitchen makeover](/post/rental-kitchen-makeover/) apply — test on a hidden patch, leave it a week, and peel it slowly to see what comes away with it. Over months the adhesive bonds harder, and a kitchen is warm, which accelerates that.

## Replacement doors

If the carcasses are sound and only the doors are tired, new doors on the existing boxes cost a fraction of a new kitchen. It is also the only option that changes the *design* rather than the colour — a route out of a door profile you dislike, or doors swollen at the bottom from a leak.

Measure every door and drawer front individually. Kitchens are rarely as consistent as they look.

## While the doors are off

The best moment to do the things that are awkward later:

- Line the shelves.
- Fit under-cabinet lighting.
- Re-sort the contents properly — [small kitchen organization ideas](/post/small-kitchen-organization-ideas/) covers grouping by use.
- Paint the inside of one cabinet a different colour, if you are feeling cheerful about it.
- Decide whether one run would be better without doors at all — [a kitchen with no upper cabinets](/post/kitchen-with-no-upper-cabinets/) has the storage arithmetic.

## What goes wrong

- **Not degreasing.** The commonest cause of peeling, by a distance.
- **Wall emulsion on cabinets.** It never hardens enough and marks permanently.
- **No adhesion primer on laminate.** The paint lifts at the edges within months.
- **Thick coats to save time.** Three thin ones, always.
- **Rehanging too early.** Touch-dry is not cured.
- **Buying handles before measuring the centres.** 96, 128 or 160 mm.
- **Film on profiled doors.** It will not sit into the moulding.
