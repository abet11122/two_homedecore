Most renter decorating advice stops at "use adhesive strips", which is where the problems start. They work, within limits, and the limits are narrower than the packaging suggests. Here is what actually holds, on what, and how to get it off again.

## Weigh it first

This is the step everyone skips. Put the frame, mirror or shelf on kitchen scales.

Adhesive strip ratings vary enormously by product — from about half a kilogram up to around seven — and every rating assumes ideal conditions: a clean, smooth, fully cured surface and correct application. Choose a rating comfortably **above** the item's weight, not equal to it.

Above roughly 3–5 kg, stop looking at the wall. Heavy mirrors, loaded shelves and anything you would be upset to lose belong on the floor or on freestanding furniture.

## Know which walls will betray you

The adhesive is rarely the failure point. The wall's own surface is.

| Surface | Risk | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gloss or satin paint, fully cured | Low | The best case |
| Matt emulsion | Medium | The commonest rental finish, and the commonest casualty |
| Freshly painted (under about 4 weeks) | High | Paint is still curing; it will lift |
| Textured or woodchip | High | Not enough flat contact; it grips the texture and tears it |
| Wallpaper | Very high | Takes the paper off, every time |
| Bathroom and kitchen walls | Variable | Steam and grease break the bond early |

Whatever the surface, do a hidden test: stick one strip somewhere nobody looks, leave it **at least a week**, then remove it properly and see what comes away. A week is the minimum because adhesive bonds continue to strengthen for days.

## The methods, roughly by how much they hold

**Tension rods.** The most underrated thing on this list. A spring-loaded rod braced between two surfaces, no fixings at all. Curtains inside a window reveal, a clothes rail across an alcove, a rod under the sink for spray bottles, a short rod in a kitchen recess for utensils. The constraint is that it needs two opposing surfaces close enough together, so it suits recesses, alcoves and reveals rather than open walls.

For curtains it has one real drawback: a tension rod has to sit inside the reveal, so you lose the trick of hanging high and wide. [How high to hang curtains](/post/how-high-to-hang-curtains/) explains what that costs.

**Over-door hooks.** Hook over the top of any standard door. Coats, bags, towels, a hanging organiser. No damage at all, though check the door closes properly afterwards — some hooks foul the frame.

**Picture ledges.** A narrow shelf held on adhesive strips, with frames **leaning** on it rather than hung. The ledge carries far less load than the frames would individually, and you can rearrange without touching the wall again.

**Leaning.** A large framed piece or mirror resting against the wall on the floor, a mantel or a sideboard. Zero risk, looks deliberate, and it is what [what to put on an empty wall](/post/what-to-put-on-an-empty-wall/) recommends for anything heavy.

**Freestanding furniture as vertical surface.** A bookcase, a shelf unit, a clothes rail, a trolley. Instead of asking the wall to hold things, bring in something that holds things itself. This is the answer for storage generally — [bedroom with no closet](/post/bedroom-with-no-closet/) works through it for clothes.

**Adhesive hooks and strips.** For light frames, hooks and small objects, within the limits above.

**Removable wallpaper and film.** Genuinely transformative on one wall, and genuinely risky on the wrong surface. Same rules as everything else: test patch, a week's wait, written agreement from the landlord for anything large. ## Applying them so they hold

1. **Clean with rubbing alcohol**, not a household spray. Kitchen cleaners leave a residue that stops adhesion — this single step prevents most early failures.
2. **Let it dry completely.**
3. **Press firmly for the time the instructions say**, usually around 30 seconds, and press the strip against the wall on its own first.
4. **Wait before loading.** Most products want an hour before anything hangs on them.
5. **Keep a spare strip** of each type, with the packaging, for when one lets go.

## Taking them off

The technique matters more than the product.

Find the tab and **pull slowly, straight down, parallel to the wall**. Stretching the strip is what breaks the bond. Pulling outwards is what takes a disc of paint with it.

If there is no tab, or it snaps: warm the area gently with a hairdryer on low, then work a length of dental floss behind the strip and saw it free.

Residue comes off with an adhesive remover suited to the surface — tested somewhere hidden first.

## Before you stick anything

Photograph every wall when you move in, so there is a record of what was already marked. Keep the landlord's written permission for anything larger than a few hooks. And check the lease: some prohibit adhesives explicitly, which is worth knowing before rather than after.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Not weighing the item.** Ratings are limits, not suggestions.
- **Sticking to fresh paint.** Four weeks minimum.
- **Household cleaner instead of alcohol.** Residue breaks the bond.
- **Pulling outwards to remove.** Straight down, slowly.
- **Anything heavy on adhesive.** Over 3–5 kg, use the floor.
- **Skipping the test patch.** A week tells you what a minute cannot.
