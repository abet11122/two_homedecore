Everything else in a bedroom arranges itself around the bed, so this is the only decision that really matters. Get it wrong and you spend the next two years turning sideways to get past a chest of drawers.

## Start with four clearances

These are the numbers that rule out most arrangements before you try them.

| Where | Clearance | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Side used by one person | 60 cm (24 in) | Getting in and out, and making the bed |
| Side used occasionally | 45 cm (18 in) | Absolute minimum to pass sideways |
| In front of a wardrobe or chest | 90 cm (36 in) | Door or drawer fully open, plus you |
| Foot of the bed, if you walk past | 60 cm (24 in) | A route, not a squeeze |

Measure your room, draw it roughly to scale, and put the bed in. Most small rooms will only accept two or three arrangements once these are applied — which makes the decision much easier than staring at an empty room does.

## Then pick the wall

**Opposite the door is usually right.** You see the bed square-on when you walk in, the room reads as deliberate, and the circulation stays at the edges. It is the default for a reason.

**The wall the door swings onto is usually wrong.** Either the door hits the bed or you lose the clearance behind it. Map the swing before anything else; this is the one constraint that cannot be negotiated with.

**A corner saves the most floor.** Headboard on one wall, one long side against another. For a single bed or a child's room this is often the best use of a small room, and it frees a genuine play or desk area. [Small boys bedroom ideas](/post/small-boys-bedroom-ideas/) uses the same logic.

For a double, the corner has a cost: whoever sleeps on the inside climbs over the other person. Fine for one sleeper, tiring for two. Decide which problem you would rather have.

**Centred on the longest free wall** gives the most balanced room if the clearances allow it, because you can have a bedside table on both sides.

## The awkward cases

**Under a window.** Common in small rooms, and perfectly workable. Three things to plan for:

- A cold draught in winter, especially with single glazing. A headboard or a bolster helps.
- The curtains. Either short ones that stop clear of the mattress, or full-length ones hung wide enough that they fall beside the bed rather than across it. [How high to hang curtains](/post/how-high-to-hang-curtains/) covers the rod width that makes the second option work.
- The radiator, which is usually under the window and which the bed will largely block.

**Against a radiator.** Mostly a waste of heat rather than a hazard — the bed absorbs it and the room warms slowly. Leave a gap where the layout allows, keep bedding off the radiator, and check the manufacturer's clearance guidance.

**Under a sloped ceiling.** Put the bed where the ceiling is lowest. It is the one piece of furniture you use lying down, so it is the only thing that does not need head height. Measure the height at the headboard, though, so sitting up in bed does not involve hitting the plaster.

**A room with two doors,** or a door and a loft hatch. Map every swing, then place the bed in whatever is left.

## Then the bedside tables

Once the bed has a position, see what is left beside it. If there is less than about 40 cm, a conventional nightstand will not fit and you want one of the alternatives — a wall-mounted shelf, a clamp-on tray, a slim stool. [Bedside table alternatives](/post/bedside-table-alternatives/) runs through the options and their dimensions.

A bed pushed into a corner only gets one bedside surface. That is worth knowing before you commit, not after.

## Test it with paper

Cut a newspaper rectangle the exact footprint of the bed — including the headboard depth, which people forget — and lay it on the floor. Add rectangles for the wardrobe and the chest.

Walk the room. Open every door. Pretend to make the bed. It takes fifteen minutes and saves moving a divan base twice.

## Common layout mistakes

- **Ignoring the door swing.** It is the one thing you cannot change.
- **Less than 45 cm on an access side.** You will resent it daily.
- **Blocking a wardrobe.** 90 cm in front, or the doors do not open.
- **A double in a corner for two sleepers.** One person climbs over the other.
- **Forgetting the headboard depth.** It can add 10–15 cm to the footprint.
- **Placing the bed before measuring.** Draw it first.
