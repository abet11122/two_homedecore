Before buying a single lamp, go and look at the curtains. In most dark rooms, a third of the window is covered by fabric all day, and getting it off the glass costs nothing. That is the first move, and it is bigger than almost anything you can buy.

After that, brightening a room is three things in order: how much daylight gets in, how much of it the surfaces give back, and what you do after dark.

## 1. Let the window be the size it is

Curtains stacked over the glass are the commonest cause of an unnecessarily dark room.

Move the rod so it extends **15–25 cm past the frame on each side**, and the open curtains sit on wall instead of window. On a 120 cm window that can be a quarter of the glass returned. [How high to hang curtains](/post/how-high-to-hang-curtains/) has the full set of measurements.

Then check the rest of the opening:

- **Clear the reveal.** Objects on the sill, a plant on the glass, a blind half-lowered out of habit.
- **Clean the glass, inside and out.** Unglamorous; genuinely measurable on a grimy window.
- **Cut back what is outside.** A branch or an overgrown shrub against the window takes more light than anything inside the room is going to give back.
- **Swap heavy linings for sheers** where privacy allows, or hang both so you can choose.

## 2. Make the surfaces give light back

A room is bright in proportion to what its surfaces reflect, and paint brands publish that number: **light reflectance value**, or LRV, from 0 (absolute black) to 100 (perfect white).

Anything **above 70** is a bright wall. Below 40 and the wall is absorbing most of what reaches it.

The thing people get wrong is reaching for the whitest white. A cool brilliant white has a high LRV but no warmth, and in a north-facing room — where the daylight is already slightly blue — it reads grey and clinical. A warm off-white at LRV 78 will almost always feel brighter than a cool white at LRV 88.

Some other surface decisions that matter more than they sound:

**The ceiling.** The largest unbroken surface in the room and the one that bounces light down onto everything. Keep it the lightest thing in the room.

**The finish.** Matt absorbs, eggshell and satin reflect. A dark hallway in matt emulsion is working against you.

**The floor.** A dark floor in a dark room is a lot of square metres doing nothing. A pale rug is the cheap version of fixing it — [what size rug do you need](/post/what-size-rug-do-i-need/) has the sizing.

**Mirrors, placed deliberately.** On the wall *beside* the window — perpendicular to it — a mirror catches daylight at an angle and spreads it into the room. Directly opposite the window it mostly reflects the view back out. Opposite a lamp, it reliably doubles the apparent light in the evening.

## 3. Build the evening light

Once the sun is down, the room is only as good as its lamps, and one bright ceiling fitting is the worst possible answer. It flattens everything and throws shadows upward.

Use **three sources at three heights**:

- High — the ceiling fitting, on a dimmer, used as fill rather than as the main event.
- Middle — a table lamp on a side table, a sideboard, or a shelf, at roughly seated eye level.
- Low or indirect — a floor lamp, a wall light, or a lamp aimed at a wall or ceiling so the light arrives bounced.

Two bulb specifications are worth caring about:

**Colour temperature.** 2700K is warm and suits living rooms and bedrooms. 3000K is slightly crisper. Above 4000K reads as office lighting and will make a cold room colder.

**CRI.** Colour rendering index, 0–100. Below 80 and colours go muddy — wood looks grey, skin looks ill. Look for **CRI 90+**. It costs a little more and it is the difference between a room that is lit and a room that looks good lit.

## The case for giving up and going dark

Sometimes the honest answer is that the room has almost no daylight and never will, and that painting it pale is a fight you lose. A box room, an internal hallway, a basement.

Painted a deep warm colour — olive, oxblood, a proper dark green — and lit with several warm lamps, that room stops being a failed bright room and becomes a deliberately cosy one. ## What not to do

- **Cool white bulbs to "add light".** They add glare and remove warmth.
- **One overhead fitting.** Three sources at three heights.
- **Brilliant white paint in a north-facing room.** It goes grey. Warm the white.
- **A mirror straight opposite the window.** Try the perpendicular wall instead.
- **Heavy curtains that stack on the glass.** Move the rod.
- **Dark floor, dark rug, dark walls, then one lamp.** Pick which surfaces are doing the reflecting.
