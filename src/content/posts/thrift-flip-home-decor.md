A good second-hand piece is better made than most of what you can buy new at the same price, and a bad one is a skip run you pay to collect. The difference comes down to materials and five minutes of checking.

## What flips well

**Solid wood anything.** The clearest signal of a piece worth the effort. It can be sanded, painted, oiled, cut down and repaired. Check the end grain on a drawer front or the underside of a top — if the grain runs continuously around an edge, it is solid; if it stops at a thin layer, it is veneer.

**Lamps.** The easiest flip there is. Charity shops are full of good bases under dated shades. New shade, paint the base, and the change is disproportionate to the effort. If the cord is cracked or the plug is old, have it rewired or replace the flex — treat anything electrical cautiously and get a competent person to look at it if you are unsure.

**Mirrors and picture frames.** The frame is the value. Paint it, re-glaze it, leave the mirror foxed if the spotting looks deliberate.

**Metal furniture.** Garden chairs, filing cabinets, trolleys, bed frames. Rust is almost always surface-level and treatable, and metal takes spray paint well.

**Glass-topped tables.** The glass is the expensive part and it is usually fine. Bases paint easily.

**Baskets, ceramics, wooden boxes.** Cheap, instantly usable, no work required.

**Dining chairs.** Especially a mismatched set, which looks deliberate rather than makeshift once they share a paint colour or a seat fabric.

## What to walk away from

**Veneered chipboard and MDF.** The thin veneer cannot be sanded, and the board underneath swells permanently at the first hint of damp. Flat-pack furniture that has already been assembled and moved once is usually at the end of its life.

**Upholstered furniture.** Sofas, armchairs, padded headboards. Smells and pests are hard to assess, and fabric holds both. If you do look, inspect the seams and the underside in good light.

**Anything with small round holes.** Could be old woodworm damage, could be active. Fresh, pale dust around the holes suggests active. Not a risk worth taking into a flat with other wooden furniture.

**Mattresses.** Always.

**Anything with a missing structural part.** A drawer runner is fixable. A missing leg on a cast frame is not.

## The five checks, in order

1. **Rock it.** Hold a corner and push gently. A wobble means loose joints — fixable on solid wood, usually terminal on chipboard.
2. **Open everything.** Every drawer, every door. Runners that stick, hinges that droop, drawers that drop at the front when open.
3. **Look underneath.** Damp staining, a tide mark, small round holes, a repair someone hid.
4. **Smell it.** Properly, close up. Damp and smoke are extremely difficult to get out of wood and impossible out of fabric.
5. **Check it is standing honestly.** Pieces get positioned to hide a missing foot or a split side.

Then measure — the piece, your doorway, your stairs, and the space it is going into. A tape measure in a bag is what stops the expensive mistake.

## Before you paint anything old

Two things worth knowing.

**Old paint.** Domestic lead paint was restricted decades ago, but older furniture can still carry it, and sanding turns it into dust you breathe. When a finish is old and unknown, do not dry-sand it. Check the current guidance for your country, and prefer a liquid deglosser or a primer formulated to bond over existing paint.

**Preparation decides the result.** Degrease, key the surface, prime for the material you have, then two or three thin coats of a paint made for furniture rather than walls. The full sequence — and why skipping it causes peeling — is in [how to update kitchen cabinets cheaply](/post/how-to-update-kitchen-cabinets-cheap/); the same method applies to any furniture.

## Flips that are mostly not painting

Paint is the obvious move and often not the best one.

- **New hardware.** Replacing knobs and handles on an old chest changes it completely. Measure the hole centres first — commonly 96, 128 or 160 mm.
- **Oil instead of paint.** A tired solid wood top usually wants a light sand and an oil, not six hours of brushwork.
- **A new shade.** On a lamp, this is the whole job.
- **Cut it down.** A too-tall bookcase shortened by one shelf fits where it did not.
- **Use it for something else.** A small chest as a bedside table, a stool as a plant stand — [bedside table alternatives](/post/bedside-table-alternatives/) is full of pieces doing other jobs.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Buying veneered board because it looks solid.** Check the end grain.
- **Skipping the smell test.** Damp and smoke do not come out.
- **Dry-sanding an unknown old finish.** Deglosser or bonding primer instead.
- **No tape measure.** The doorway is the constraint, not the room.
- **Wall paint on furniture.** It never hardens properly and marks.
- **Buying a project you will not start.** One piece at a time.
