Start with the bed. It's the biggest piece in a studio, so it goes down first and everything else — furniture that folds, extends or stores — fits around it. Keep walkways clear, leave the window wall open, and give each zone its own light. A 25 m² studio can feel like a one-bedroom when every piece earns its floor space.

The furniture comes first below, then how to build boundaries between zones without building walls, then four worked layouts.

## Start With the Bed

The bed decides the layout. Choose the type that matches how you live:

| Bed type | Footprint | Best for | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daybed (single) | About 90 × 200 cm | Sofa by day, single bed by night | Single-width sleeping |
| Sofa bed | Sofa footprint; about 140 × 200 cm open | Occasional guests | Less comfortable every night |
| Wall (Murphy) bed | 30–45 cm deep closed | Freeing the whole floor by day | Needs a solid wall; costly |
| Raised or loft bed | Bed footprint, storage or desk below | High ceilings (270 cm+) | Climbing; less headroom |
| Standard double or queen | 140–160 × 200 cm | Couples; best sleep | Takes the most floor |

If you sleep in the studio every night, a proper bed usually beats a sofa bed. Put it in the corner furthest from the front door, ideally with its long side against a wall, and screen it from the living area.

## Rank the Zones by Waking Hours

Before placing anything but the bed, write down what the room has to do. For most studios that is sleep, sit, eat and, only if you genuinely work from home, work. Don't build a desk zone you use twice a month.

Then rank the zones by waking hours per week. The zone with the most hours gets the window and the best position. The bed, used with your eyes shut, can take the darkest corner, where it will sleep better anyway. If you work from home eight hours a day, the desk gets the window.

## Build Boundaries Without Walls

A studio rarely fails for lack of floor area. It fails because every activity leaks into every other one: you eat where you work and sleep where you entertain, and nothing in the room signals that one thing has ended. Five tools fix that, cheapest first.

1. **Rugs.** One rug per zone, with bare floor between them. The gap is the boundary. In the seating zone, at least the front legs of every seat sit on the rug. Vary the texture between zones, a flat-weave under the table and something with pile under the sofa.
2. **The backs of furniture.** A sofa facing into the seating zone, its back towards the bed, is a wall. A 25–30 cm console behind it strengthens the line and adds a surface. An open-backed shelf unit does the same job and still passes light, which matters if it sits between the window and the rest of the room.
3. **Height.** A tall shelf at the edge of the sleeping zone, low furniture in the seating zone, and a pendant hung 75–85 cm above the dining table. The pendant draws a vertical boundary that nothing at floor level can.
4. **Light on separate switches.** The strongest of the five, because you can turn a zone off. When the desk lamp goes off at night, the work zone stops existing.
5. **Curtains, last.** A ceiling-track curtain closes fully and disappears when open, but it costs light and makes the room read smaller. Use one when the bed is visible from the front door and that bothers you.

Most good dividers are furniture you were going to own anyway, turned ninety degrees.

## Choose Furniture That Does Two Jobs

### 1. A compact sofa on legs

A 2-seater of 150–180 cm fits most studios. Visible legs show floor beneath and make the room feel bigger. Watch the overall depth more than the width: 80–85 cm front to back instead of 95 cm or more buys circulation space, which is what actually makes a small room feel liveable.

### 2. A table that folds or extends

- **Drop-leaf or gateleg table:** 25–40 cm deep folded, seats four open.
- **Round table, 70–80 cm:** easier to walk around than a square one.
- **Wall-mounted fold-down table:** no footprint when closed.
- **Extending coffee table:** some lift up to dining height.

### 3. An ottoman or bench that stores

A storage ottoman is a footrest, extra seat and blanket box. A bench at the end of the bed holds bedding and gives you somewhere to sit.

### 4. Nesting or C-shaped side tables

C-shaped tables slide over the sofa arm or bed edge. Nesting tables tuck into one footprint and pull out when guests come.

### 5. Folding chairs on a hook

Two folding chairs hung on a wall hook give you extra seating without floor space.

## Store Up and Out of Sight

- **Tall, narrow storage:** a 180–200 cm bookcase or wardrobe uses height, not floor. Anchor it to the wall.
- **Under the bed:** drawers or an ottoman base often replace a chest of drawers.
- **Closed storage at eye level:** doors or baskets on open shelving keep the room calm.
- **Above the kitchen cabinets and wardrobe:** lidded boxes for seasonal things.
- **Behind the front door:** hooks and an over-door rack.

## Four Sample Layouts

**25 m² (270 sq ft):** Daybed against the long wall as sofa and bed. A drop-leaf table under the window with two folding chairs. A tall wardrobe beside the kitchen. One rug under the daybed and coffee table.

**32 m² (345 sq ft), one window wall, working from home:** Desk across the window, side-on so the light comes from the left. Sofa with its back to the desk, facing into the room on a 200 × 290 cm rug; the sofa back is the wall between work and living. Bed on a low frame in the far, darkest corner, with one tall shelf at its foot and a wall sconce instead of a bedside table. A small round table near the kitchen under a low pendant, on a flat-weave rug. A clear 90 cm run from the front door past the sofa to the kitchen, and 60 cm around three sides of the bed. Four zones, no divider bought.

**35 m² (375 sq ft):** Double bed in the far corner, screened by an open bookcase. A 2-seater sofa facing away from the bed, with its back acting as a divider. A 75 cm round table near the kitchen. A rug for the living zone, a runner by the bed.

**45 m² (485 sq ft):** Queen bed behind a ceiling-track curtain. A full sofa and armchair around a coffee table. A small desk in an alcove or at the end of the bed. Separate rugs and lighting for each zone.

## Keep Walkways and the Window Clear

- Keep the main route, from the front door to the rest of the room, **75–90 cm** wide, and every other daily route at least **60 cm**. Below 60 cm people turn sideways to pass, and the room feels cramped whatever its size.
- Walk your real routes before committing: door to bed, bed to bathroom, kitchen to table.
- Don't put tall furniture in front of the window — it blocks the light the whole studio depends on.
- Make sure the front door, wardrobe doors and kitchen cupboards open fully.

## Light Each Zone Separately

A single ceiling light makes a studio feel like one flat box. Instead:

- **Living zone:** a floor lamp beside the sofa and a table lamp.
- **Sleeping zone:** a bedside lamp or plug-in wall sconce.
- **Kitchen and dining:** under-cabinet lights or a pendant over the table.
- **Work zone:** a task lamp.

Warm white (2700K) bulbs throughout. At night, turn off the overhead and switch on only the zone you're using — the room seems to change size.

## Make It Look Bigger

- Light, warm walls and one consistent color palette across the whole studio.
- One rug per zone, big enough for the furniture's front legs.
- Curtains hung high and wide, from near the ceiling and past the window frame.
- A large mirror opposite or beside the window.
- Fewer, larger pieces of art instead of many small frames.

## Reset It Every Night

Ten minutes: desk cleared, throw folded, table wiped, everything back in its zone. In a home with doors you can close a mess away. In a studio every surface is on view from every angle, so the room is only ever as calm as its worst corner. One closed storage unit per zone makes this faster, because each area can be tidied on its own.

## Studio Layout Mistakes
- **Buying a sofa before deciding where the bed goes.**
- **Tall furniture against the window.**
- **Too many small pieces.** Fewer, better-scaled pieces feel calmer.
- **Only one ceiling light.**
- **No closed storage.** Everything you own is on view.
- **The bed in the best spot by default.** Give the window to the zone with the most waking hours.
