There is a number for this, and it is 145 cm. Hang the centre of a picture that far off the floor and it will look right in almost any room, because it is where your eyes already are when you are standing up. Galleries use it. Museums use it. Most people, left to themselves, hang art about 20 cm higher than that.

## Measure to the middle

The rule governs the **centre** of the piece, not the top edge and not the hook. That sounds obvious and it is where nearly every mistake comes from.

The arithmetic:

1. Measure the picture's height. Halve it.
2. Measure from the top of the frame down to the taut hanging wire or the back hook. Call this the drop.
3. Hook height = 145 − (half the height) + the drop.

For a 60 cm tall frame with a 7 cm drop: 145 − 30 + 7 = **122 cm**. That is where the nail goes.

Do this once with a calculator and a pencil and you will never hang a picture by eye again.

## When furniture overrides the rule

The 145 cm rule assumes a bare wall. Put a sofa, a bed or a console under the picture and the furniture takes over, because what the eye reads now is the *gap*.

Leave **15–25 cm (6–10 in)** between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame.

- Below about 15 cm, the picture looks like it is sitting on the furniture.
- Above about 25 cm, it floats off on its own and the two stop looking related.

With a standard sofa back at roughly 80–85 cm, this usually lands the picture's centre somewhere near 145 cm anyway. The two rules agree more often than not. When they disagree — a high-backed sofa, a tall headboard — the gap wins.

Above a bed, the same gap applies, measured from the top of the headboard. [What to put on an empty wall](/post/what-to-put-on-an-empty-wall/) covers what actually belongs there.

## Scale: two-thirds

A picture under a sofa should be about **two-thirds the width of the sofa**. A 200 cm sofa wants 130–150 cm of art.

That can be one piece, a pair, or nine small frames — what matters is the width of the whole group, not of any one frame. A single A3 print over a three-seater is the single most recognisable sign that nobody measured anything.

The same ratio works over a console, a bed, a fireplace and a sideboard.

## Gallery walls

A gallery wall is one object. Design it as a shape first and a set of pictures second.

**Lay it out on the floor.** Arrange the whole group on the floor in front of the wall until the outline looks right. Photograph it so you can rebuild it.

**Keep the gaps even.** 5–8 cm between frames, the same everywhere. Consistent spacing is what lets mismatched frames hang together; uneven spacing makes matching frames look accidental.

**Hang the anchor first.** Pick the largest piece, put its centre at 145 cm, and build outward from it.

**Use paper.** Cut a newspaper rectangle for each frame, mark where its hook falls, and tape them up. Adjust the paper, not the wall. Then nail straight through the mark and tear the paper away.

For a group over a sofa, treat the gallery's overall rectangle as the picture: two-thirds the sofa width, 15–25 cm above the back.

## Hanging it so it stays up

Weigh the frame. A large framed piece with glass is heavier than it looks, and the difference between a 2 kg and a 7 kg picture is the difference between a pin and a proper wall fixing.

Find out what the wall is: masonry, plasterboard, or plasterboard with a stud behind it. Into a stud is always best. Into plasterboard alone, use an anchor rated above the frame's weight — the [floating shelf checklist](/post/diy-floating-shelves/) explains how to tell the wall types apart and what each one takes.

Two hooks spaced apart, rather than one in the middle, keeps a picture level and stops it drifting every time a door slams.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Measuring to the top of the frame.** It is the centre that sits at 145 cm.
- **Forgetting the wire drop.** A slack wire lifts the hook position by several centimetres.
- **Hanging too high.** If in doubt, lower. Almost no picture in a home is hung too low.
- **One small frame on a big wall.** Scale is two-thirds of the furniture.
- **Uneven gallery spacing.** Pick a gap and hold it.
- **Adhesive strips on a textured or newly painted wall.** They take the paint with them.
