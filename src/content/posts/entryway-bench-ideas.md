A bench is the piece of entryway furniture people most want and most often buy in the wrong size. The seat ends up too deep, the hall ends up too narrow, and the bench becomes a shelf for parcels that nobody has ever sat on.

Four measurements decide whether it works.

## The four numbers

**Seat height: 42–48 cm.** The same range as a dining chair, for the same reasons. Below 40 cm, standing up again is genuinely difficult for anyone with stiff knees. Above 50 cm, children and shorter adults perch with their feet off the floor, which is no use for putting shoes on.

**Seat depth: 35–40 cm.** Enough to sit properly, shallow enough to stay out of the way. Go deeper and two things happen: the bench eats the hall, and it becomes a surface people put bags on rather than a seat.

**Clear floor in front: about 90 cm.** This is the one people skip. You are not just sitting — you are bending to a lace, or lifting a foot onto the opposite knee. Sixty centimetres lets you sit; ninety lets you deal with boots.

Measure it **with the front door open**, because that is when the hall is narrowest and that is exactly when you will be using the bench.

**Width: 45–60 cm per person.** One person needs 45–60 cm, two need 100–120 cm. A bench longer than the wall it sits against looks wrong and gets used as storage.

## Choosing the type

**A plain bench.** Lightest visually, cheapest, and easiest to fit. No storage, which in a small hall is a real cost.

**A bench with open shelving underneath.** The common choice. Shoes go below, people sit on top. Check the clear height under the seat — most give 25–30 cm, which takes trainers but not boots. [Shoe storage for a small entryway](/post/shoe-storage-small-entryway/) has the depths and heights that matter.

**A bench with closed storage or a lift-up lid.** Hides the mess, which matters in a hall you see from the living room. Two cautions: a lift-up lid means clearing the seat before you can get into it, so it tends to hold things you need rarely, and closed storage is wrong for wet shoes.

**A storage box or ottoman.** Not a bench, does the job. Often cheaper, and it moves if the layout changes.

**A built-in run.** Bench, hooks and a shelf above, fitted to the wall. The best use of a narrow hall if you own the place, because it can be made exactly the depth the hall can spare.

## Hooks above

A bench and hooks belong together — it is the sitting-down-to-take-off-boots-while-hanging-up-a-coat corner.

- **165–175 cm** from the floor for adults.
- **100–120 cm** for children, so they can hang their own coat rather than handing it over.
- **At least 50 cm** between the top of the seat and the lowest hook, or coats hang into the space your head occupies.
- **15–20 cm apart**, or coats bunch into each other.

A shelf above the hooks at around 180–190 cm takes hats, gloves and the things that otherwise live on the bench.

## When the hall cannot take a bench

Plenty of halls cannot give up 40 cm of depth and 90 cm of clearance. That does not mean standing on one leg forever.

**A wall-mounted folding seat.** Folds flat to about 10 cm when it is not in use. The obvious answer for a narrow hall, and the one most people do not think of. Needs a proper wall fixing — the [floating shelf checklist](/post/diy-floating-shelves/) covers identifying the wall and choosing the anchor, and a seat carries far more load than a shelf.

**A wide step stool.** Tucks under a console or against the wall, pulls out when needed, costs very little.

**A single chair.** Pulled slightly out of the route, used for sitting and for the coat you have not hung up yet. Less tidy than a bench, more useful than nothing.

**Nothing, and a different place to sit.** If the hall opens into a room with a chair two steps away, use that. Not every entryway has to do everything — [when there is no entryway at all](/post/no-entryway-apartment-ideas/) takes that further.

## Keeping it a seat

The failure mode of every entryway bench is the same: it fills with bags, post and parcels, and stops being somewhere to sit.

Two things help. Give the items that colonise it their own home — a tray for post, a hook for bags. And keep one cushion on the seat, because a surface that obviously belongs to sitting gets used for sitting.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Too deep.** 35–40 cm. A 50 cm bench is a shelf.
- **No clearance in front.** 90 cm, measured with the door open.
- **Hooks too low.** Coats end up in your face when you sit.
- **Closed storage for wet shoes.** Use an open tray instead.
- **A lift-up lid you have to clear first.** It will stop being opened.
- **Buying for the wall length rather than the person.** 45–60 cm each.
