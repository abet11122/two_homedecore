The fear of mixing woods comes from furniture showrooms, where everything is sold as a matching set. Real rooms accumulate: an inherited table, a second-hand chair, the floor that came with the flat. Rooms that look collected rather than bought are almost always mixing three or four timbers, and they do it on purpose.

There are three things to get right, and one trap.

## 1. Undertone, not colour

This is the whole game. Woods do not need to be the same shade; they need to lean the same way.

Hold a sheet of plain white printer paper against the wood in daylight. The white gives your eye a fixed reference and the bias shows up immediately:

- **Warm** — red, orange or yellow against the white. Cherry, teak, mahogany, most pine, honey oak.
- **Cool** — grey, violet or ashy. Grey-washed oak, some walnut, limed ash, driftwood finishes.
- **Neutral** — neither. Untreated white oak, maple, much new birch ply.

A dark warm walnut and a pale warm oak will sit together comfortably despite being nothing like the same colour, because they are leaning the same way. A warm honey pine next to a cool grey oak will fight, even though they are closer in value.

Neutral woods get on with almost anything, which is why white oak has been everywhere for a decade.

## 2. One wood has to be in charge

Pick a dominant tone and let it hold about **two-thirds** of the wood in the room. Usually that choice is made for you — the floor is the largest wooden surface and you are probably not replacing it.

Everything else is a guest. The dominant wood sets the undertone, and the guests should agree with it.

This is why the instinctive move of buying a coffee table that *nearly* matches the floor backfires. You end up with two pieces competing for the lead role, neither clearly dominant.

## 3. Repeat every tone

A single piece in a tone that appears nowhere else looks like it wandered in. The fix is repetition: **each tone should show up at least twice**, in different places and ideally at different scales.

If the dining table is walnut, find walnut again — a picture frame, a bowl, the legs of a lamp, a shelf. It does not need to be a large object. It needs to be a second sighting, so the eye reads a scheme rather than an accident.

This is the trick that does most of the work, and it is the one people skip because it feels too small to matter.

## The trap: nearly matching

Two woods that are obviously different look intentional. Two woods that are *almost* the same look like you tried to match them and failed.

So when you are choosing a new piece, aim either for a clear contrast with the dominant wood or for a genuine match. The uncomfortable middle — one shade off, half a step warmer — is the thing to avoid.

Where two near-matching woods already exist and you cannot change either, separate them so they are never seen touching. A rug between the floor and a table is the usual answer; [what size rug do you need](/post/what-size-rug-do-i-need/) has the sizing.

## Things that help it hold together

**A textile that contains both.** A patterned rug or cushion carrying two of your tones ties them to each other retrospectively.

**Consistent metal.** Brass throughout, or black throughout. One consistent metal across a room forgives a lot of variation in the wood.

**Grain contrast.** A heavily grained oak beside a smooth, closed-grain maple reads as deliberate variety. Two different woods with identical grain patterns read as a failed match.

**Warm light.** 2700K bulbs pull every wood in the room slightly towards each other. Cool bulbs separate them and make an orange floor look more orange. [How to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) covers bulb choice in more detail.

**Black.** A black-framed picture, black lamp or black chair gives the eye a fixed point and makes the wood variation read as a palette.

## Working with a floor you did not choose

Most people's dominant wood is a rental floor they would not have picked.

**Orange pine or honey laminate.** Lean into the warmth: cream, terracotta, olive, brass. Fighting it with cool grey makes the orange shout. A large rug removes most of it from view.

**Grey-washed or very cool oak.** Cool-leaning woods and cooler neutrals work; a single warm wood will look stranded unless you repeat it.

**Dark brown laminate.** Treat it almost as a neutral and get the light right, since a dark floor absorbs a lot of what the room has.

**Mid oak.** You have drawn the easy one. Almost anything with a warm or neutral undertone will sit with it.

## Common mistakes

- **Matching colour instead of undertone.** The paper test takes ten seconds.
- **Buying a near-match to the floor.** Contrast clearly or match exactly.
- **One orphan tone.** Repeat every wood at least twice.
- **Four or more tones with nothing holding them.** Three is the comfortable ceiling.
- **Cool bulbs over warm wood.** It exaggerates every orange in the room.
- **Mixed metals on top of mixed woods.** Pick one metal and stay with it.
