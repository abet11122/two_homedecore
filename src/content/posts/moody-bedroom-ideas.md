The usual advice is that small or dark bedrooms should be painted pale to open them up. For a room with reasonable daylight that is sound. For a north-facing box room with one small window, it produces a grey, flat space that never quite works — because white needs light to reflect, and there isn't any.

Those are exactly the rooms a moody scheme suits.

## Which rooms are good candidates

**Good:** north-facing rooms, rooms with one small window, rooms you mostly use after dark, rooms with an awkward shape where dark paint hides the irregularities, and rooms where the view out is poor.

**Less good:** a bright south-facing room you use during the day, a room with a genuinely good view, and any room where you need to see colour accurately — getting dressed in a dark room needs a well-lit mirror elsewhere.

## Paint everything, not just the walls

This is the decision that separates a dark room that works from one that looks half-done.

Dark walls with a brilliant white ceiling, white skirting and a white door create hard edges at every boundary. The eye reads those edges, measures the room by them, and the result feels like a small box someone painted dark.

**Carry the colour onto the ceiling, the skirting, the architrave and ideally the door.** With the edges gone, the boundaries blur and the room reads as enveloping. It is the same principle as in [how to make a bedroom look bigger](/post/how-to-make-a-bedroom-look-bigger/) — contrast, not colour, is what shrinks a room.

If painting the ceiling feels like a step too far, a tone or two lighter than the walls in the same colour works. Brilliant white does not.

## Choose a warm dark, not a cold one

Deep green, ink blue, charcoal, aubergine, oxblood, dark olive. All work. What decides whether the result feels intimate or chilly is the undertone.

A green with some yellow in it, a blue leaning slightly warm or grey, a brown-black rather than a blue-black. Pure cool darks can read as severe, particularly in a north-facing room where the daylight is already cool.

Test large samples — A4 at minimum — on the darkest wall, and look at them under your own bulbs at night as well as in daylight. Dark colours shift far more between light sources than pale ones do. The testing method in [small bathroom paint colors](/post/small-bathroom-paint-colors/) applies here too.

## Light it properly, or it is just dark

A dark room needs **more** light sources than a pale one, because the walls absorb rather than bounce. The single central bulb that was adequate before will not be.

- **Two bedside sources**, ideally wall-mounted to free the surface. [Bedside table alternatives](/post/bedside-table-alternatives/) covers the options where there is no room for a lamp.
- **Something low or indirect** — a floor lamp aimed at a wall, a lamp on a chest.
- **The ceiling fitting on a dimmer**, used as fill rather than as the main event.
- **2700K, CRI 90 or above.** In a dark room the colour quality of the light matters more, because there is less of it and everything you see is reflected off a deep colour.

[How to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) covers layering in general; the same structure applies, just with the expectation that the room stays dark on purpose.

## Texture is what stops it feeling flat

A dark room with smooth walls, flat paint and plain bedding is a cave. The same room with texture everywhere is enveloping.

- **A finish with some sheen** — eggshell rather than dead matt — catches light and gives the walls depth.
- **Layered bedding**: linen, a heavier quilt, a wool throw. Three textures minimum.
- **A headboard in fabric or cane**, which breaks up the darkest plane in the room.
- **Natural materials** — wood, rattan, leather, brass — to warm the scheme.
- **A rug with pile**, if the floor is hard.

Velvet is the obvious reach and it works, but it is better as an accent than as the whole scheme.

## Where the light goes in

One thing to keep pale or reflective: the area around the window, and anything that bounces daylight. A mirror on the wall beside the window spreads what daylight there is. Curtains in a lighter tone than the walls stop the window reading as another dark rectangle — and hung high and wide, they keep the glass clear, which matters more here than in a pale room. [How high to hang curtains](/post/how-high-to-hang-curtains/) covers how far past the frame the rod should run.

## Where it goes wrong

- **White ceiling and trim.** The commonest reason a dark room looks unfinished.
- **A cold dark.** Check the undertone before committing.
- **Dead matt paint on every surface.** Some sheen gives depth.
- **The same lighting as before.** Dark walls absorb; add sources.
- **Flat textiles.** Three textures on the bed, minimum.
- **Testing a small chip in daylight.** Large sample, your own bulbs, at night.
