Almost nothing on this list is about money. Rooms that look expensive are mostly rooms where somebody checked a few measurements and turned the big light off. Here is what is actually doing it, roughly in order of how much each one changes.

## 1. The lighting

One bright bulb in the ceiling, probably cool-toned. It flattens everything, throws shadows upward, and makes every surface in the room look like itself only worse.

Replace it with **three sources at three heights**: a dimmed ceiling fitting, a table lamp at seated eye level, a floor lamp or something indirect. Use **2700K** bulbs with a **CRI of 90 or above** — high-CRI light is what makes wood look like wood and fabric look like fabric.

This is the biggest single change available to you and it costs about the price of two lamps. [How to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) goes further into it.

## 2. The curtains

Three separate mistakes usually happening at once: hung on the window frame instead of above it, no wider than the glass, and stopping somewhere short of the floor.

Rod **10–15 cm above the frame**, extended **15–25 cm past it on each side**, curtains long enough to **reach the floor**. Total fabric width **2–2.5 times** the rod. Nothing on this list changes a room's proportions more for less money, and moving an existing rod is free. [How high to hang curtains](/post/how-high-to-hang-curtains/) has the detail.

## 3. The rug is too small

A rug floating in the middle of the floor with all the furniture stranded around it. This is the most common furnishing error there is.

The rug should at minimum catch the **front legs of every seat**. If it does not, it is reading as a bath mat in a living room. Going up a size is the single most reliable way to make a seating group look intentional — [what size rug do you need](/post/what-size-rug-do-i-need/) has the measurements.

## 4. Everything is pushed against the walls

The instinct in a small room is to shove all the furniture to the edges to leave space in the middle. It makes the room feel like a waiting area and, oddly, smaller rather than larger.

Pull the sofa **5–10 cm off the wall**. Angle a chair. Let a piece of furniture define the edge of a zone rather than following the architecture. [How to arrange furniture in a small room](/post/how-to-arrange-furniture-in-a-small-room/) covers it properly.

## 5. The art is too small and too high

A single A4 frame hung at head height over a three-seater sofa. The piece should be about **two-thirds the width of the furniture**, with **15–25 cm** between the top of the sofa and the bottom of the frame.

If one big piece is out of budget, a group of smaller frames arranged to occupy that rectangle does the same job. [How high to hang art](/post/how-high-to-hang-art/) and [what to put on an empty wall](/post/what-to-put-on-an-empty-wall/) between them cover the sizing and the options.

## 6. Too many small objects

A room with forty small decorative things on its surfaces looks cluttered whatever those things cost. The eye has nowhere to rest.

Clear one surface completely and leave it clear for a week. Then put back only what you actually missed. The usual outcome is that about a third of it stays in the box, and the room looks considerably more expensive for it.

## 7. The matching suite

Sofa, loveseat, armchair, coffee table, side tables, all from one range in one finish. It reads as a showroom display rather than a room somebody assembled.

You do not have to replace anything. Swapping one piece — a different armchair, a second-hand coffee table in another wood — breaks the set. Mixing woods deliberately is a skill with rules of its own; [how to mix wood tones](/post/how-to-mix-wood-tones/) has them.

## 8. Visible cords, and the small stuff

- Cables trailing across the floor and down the wall from the TV.
- Plastic furniture feet left on.
- Price stickers and brand tags still attached.
- Blinds hanging crooked.
- A TV mounted high above a fireplace, so everyone watches it with their chin up.

None of these are design decisions. They are all ten-minute jobs, and collectively they are doing more damage than any of them deserves.

## What is actually worth spending on

If there is some money, it goes furthest on the things that cover large areas or get touched every day:

| Worth the money | Not worth it |
| --- | --- |
| Lighting and good bulbs | Decorative objects in quantity |
| A rug big enough for the room | A rug that fits the budget but not the furniture |
| Full-length curtains, even plain ones | Short curtains in expensive fabric |
| One large piece of art | Six small ones to fill the same wall |
| Whatever you sit on daily | Matching accent furniture |

The pattern is scale and restraint. Both are free to diagnose, and most of them are free to fix.
