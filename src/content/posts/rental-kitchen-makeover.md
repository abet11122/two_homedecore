Start with the lease and a camera. Read what it actually says about fixings, adhesives and redecoration — the wording varies far more than people assume — and photograph every surface before you touch anything, including the inside of the cupboards. Then put every original handle, shelf and fitting you remove into one labelled box that goes in the back of a cupboard until you move out.

With that done, here is what is worth doing, roughly in order of how much it changes the room.

## 1. The lighting

Rented kitchens are almost universally lit by one cool-white ceiling fitting, and it is the main reason they look institutional.

Two changes:

**Swap the bulbs.** 2700–3000K, with a **CRI of 90 or above**. High-CRI light is what stops food, wood and tiles looking grey. It costs a few pounds and it is the single biggest improvement available.

**Add light under the cabinets.** A rechargeable or plug-in LED strip under the wall units lights the worktop where you actually work and makes the counter read as a surface rather than a shadow. No wiring, no fixings beyond adhesive clips.

More on bulb choice in [how to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/).

## 2. The handles

Completely reversible, surprisingly transformative, and cheap. Unscrew, keep the originals in the box, fit new ones.

The one thing to check first: **hole spacing**. Handles come in fixed centres — commonly 96, 128 and 160 mm. Measure between the centres of two existing screw holes. If you buy a different spacing you are drilling new holes in the landlord's doors, which is no longer a reversible change.

Knobs with a single screw are the safe fallback, since they fit any single hole.

## 3. Clear the worktop

Costs nothing and changes the room more than most purchases. A rented kitchen usually has less storage than you need, so things migrate onto the counter and stay there.

Work out what genuinely has to live out — kettle, perhaps a toaster — and find homes for the rest. [Small kitchen organization ideas](/post/small-kitchen-organization-ideas/) covers where the space is hiding.

## 4. Freestanding storage that leaves with you

The best money in a rental kitchen, because it is not spent on someone else's property.

- **A trolley or butcher's block** in a gap. Adds worktop and storage, and rolls out when you need the floor.
- **A slim shelf unit** in the dead space beside the fridge or at the end of a run.
- **A freestanding pantry cupboard** where there is no pantry — see [no pantry storage ideas](/post/no-pantry-storage-ideas/) for how to make one work.
- **Over-sink and over-counter racks** that stand rather than mount.
- **A rail on a tension rod** in a window recess, for utensils.

## 5. Textiles and the small stuff

A roman blind or café curtain at the window, a runner on the floor if there is room, a decent tea towel rather than a free one. In a hard room full of laminate and tile, soft materials do a disproportionate amount of work.

## 6. The cautious tier: surface coverings

This is where deposits get lost, so treat it carefully.

**Peel-and-stick film on cupboard doors or worktops** can genuinely transform a dated kitchen. It can also lift veneer, laminate or paint when it comes off — adhesives bond harder over months, and kitchens are warm, which accelerates it.

If you go ahead:

1. Get the landlord's agreement **in writing**.
2. Test on a hidden patch — inside a cupboard door, behind the bin.
3. Leave the test at least a week, then peel it slowly and look at what came with it.
4. Clean and dry the surface properly first; grease is why most films fail early.
5. Keep well clear of the hob and anywhere that gets hot.

The same logic applies to backsplashes, which have enough detail of their own: [renter-friendly backsplash ideas](/post/renter-friendly-backsplash-ideas/).

**Painting the cupboards** is a bigger step again and needs explicit permission. If you have it, [how to update kitchen cabinets cheaply](/post/how-to-update-kitchen-cabinets-cheap/) covers doing it so it lasts.

## What to spend on, and what not to

| Takes the money well | Think twice |
| --- | --- |
| Bulbs and under-cabinet lighting | Adhesive film over large areas |
| Handles (reversible, cheap) | Anything needing new holes |
| A trolley or freestanding unit | Fitted-look upgrades you leave behind |
| Storage containers and organisers | Replacing the landlord's appliances |
| Textiles and a blind | Permanent flooring |

The pattern: spend on things that move house with you, and treat anything stuck to the landlord's property as a consumable.

## Before you move out

Put the original handles back, remove films slowly and warm if needed, and compare the room with the photographs you took on day one. The box of original fittings is what makes that a ten-minute job rather than an argument.
