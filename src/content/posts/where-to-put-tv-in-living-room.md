A television is the largest black rectangle most people own, and it tends to get placed wherever the sockets already are. Two measurements and one honest decision about the fireplace will settle it better than that.

## How far away

The usable range is **1.5 to 2.5 times the screen's diagonal**.

| Screen | Diagonal | Comfortable seating distance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 43 in | 109 cm | 1.6 – 2.7 m |
| 50 in | 127 cm | 1.9 – 3.2 m |
| 55 in | 140 cm | 2.1 – 3.5 m |
| 65 in | 165 cm | 2.5 – 4.1 m |
| 75 in | 191 cm | 2.9 – 4.8 m |

Most living rooms seat people 2.5–3.5 m from the wall, which puts a 55 or 65 inch screen comfortably in range.

People routinely buy smaller than they need because they are thinking of older televisions, where sitting close meant seeing the pixels. At 4K you can sit at the near end of the range without that happening. If you are choosing between two sizes in an ordinary room, the larger one is usually the right call.

## How high

This is where most installations go wrong, and the rule is simple: **the centre of the screen belongs at seated eye level.**

For most sofas that is **100–110 cm from the floor**. Do not guess it — sit on your actual sofa, have someone measure from the floor to your eyes, and centre the screen there.

A television mounted higher makes you tilt your head back. For twenty minutes it is unnoticeable. Over a two-hour film it is a stiff neck, and it is the reason a lot of rooms with beautifully mounted TVs are not comfortable to watch anything in.

## The fireplace question

Above the fireplace is the most popular place to mount a television and usually the worst.

The problem is height. A mantel typically sits around 110–120 cm, so a screen above it centres somewhere near 150–160 cm — forty to fifty centimetres above where your eyes are. That is the chin-up position.

There is a second issue if the fire works: heat rises, and manufacturers specify operating temperature limits. Check them before committing.

Sometimes there is genuinely no alternative — one wall, one window, one chimney breast. In that case:

- Use a **pull-down mount**, which brings the screen down and forward to eye level for watching and returns it flush afterwards. It costs more than a flat bracket and it solves the problem completely.
- Or a **tilting mount**, which angles the screen down. It is a partial fix, not a complete one.
- And keep the mantel styling low and simple, so the wall is not competing with itself.

## Windows and reflections

A screen opposite a window reflects it for most of the day. A screen on the *same wall* as the window has daylight falling across it rather than into it.

So: same wall as the largest window where you can, perpendicular to it as the next best option, opposite it only if you have no choice and are prepared to use a blind.

A room with windows on three walls — common in a corner flat — usually ends up with the TV on the one solid wall, which then also decides where the sofa goes. That is fine. Let the constraint make the decision.

## Fitting it into the layout

The television is a focal point whether you want it to be or not, so arrange around it deliberately rather than apologetically. [How to arrange furniture in a small room](/post/how-to-arrange-furniture-in-a-small-room/) covers the walkway widths and the focal-point rule in full.

Two specifics worth adding here:

**Do not split the difference.** A sofa angled hopefully between the fireplace and the television serves neither and leaves unusable corners. Pick which one the seating faces.

**Deal with the cables.** Trailing cables down a wall undo an otherwise careful room instantly. A surface-mounted trunking painted the wall colour costs very little; an in-wall cable kit costs a bit more and disappears entirely. This is on the list in [why your room looks cheap](/post/why-does-my-room-look-cheap/) for a reason.

## Making it less conspicuous

You cannot make a television invisible, but you can stop it being the only thing in the room.

**A dark wall behind it.** A deep green, charcoal or navy wall absorbs the black rectangle in a way a white wall cannot. The screen stops reading as a hole.

**Build it in.** Shelving either side and above turns the TV into one element of a wall of joinery rather than an object stuck on plaster.

**Surround it with art.** Frames of similar visual weight arranged around the screen make it part of an arrangement. [What to put on an empty wall](/post/what-to-put-on-an-empty-wall/) covers the spacing.

**A media unit with some weight.** A low cabinet underneath anchors the screen and hides the boxes and cables that otherwise sit on display.

## Common mistakes

- **Mounting it too high.** Centre at seated eye level, measured from your own sofa.
- **Above a working fire without checking the heat limits.** Read the manual first.
- **Opposite a window.** Reflections all afternoon.
- **Buying small for a small room.** 4K tolerates a close seating distance.
- **Visible cables.** Trunking costs almost nothing.
- **Facing the sofa at the fireplace and the TV simultaneously.** Choose.
