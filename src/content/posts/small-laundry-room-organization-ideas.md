A small laundry area works when one load can move from dirty to put away without creating a pile on the floor. Follow a normal load through the space, find the step where it stalls — sorting, supplies, drying or folding — and fix that step first. Then check every storage idea against the appliance doors, vents and service access.

Your "laundry room" might be a closet with stacked machines, a corner of the kitchen, or a utility room. The tasks are the same; only the space changes.

## Step 1: Walk Through One Load

Take one load from start to finish and notice where things go wrong:

1. **Dirty clothes waiting** — Is there a hamper, or a pile?
2. **Sorting** — Do you actually separate loads, or wash everything together?
3. **Washing** — Can you reach detergent and stain remover without moving something?
4. **Drying** — Where do air-dry items hang?
5. **Folding** — Is there a flat surface, or does folding happen on the bed?
6. **Putting away** — Do clean clothes leave the room, or sit in a basket for days?

The stall point is where you should put your money and effort. Better shelving won't fix a drying problem.

## Step 2: Measure Around the Appliances

- **Front-loader:** allow about 105 cm (42 in) of clear floor in front so the door opens fully and you can crouch to unload.
- **Top-loader:** measure the full open height of the lid — often 90–100 cm or more from the floor. Nothing can go below that line.
- **Side gaps:** measure at the narrowest point, including hoses, plugs, skirting boards and door trim.
- **Behind the machines:** most manufacturers want a few centimetres for hoses and airflow. Vented dryers need a clear duct run.
- **Service access:** taps, shutoff valves and the drain must stay reachable.

Check your appliance manual for its required clearances — they override any rule of thumb.

## Step 3: Put Supplies Where You Use Them

| Location | Good for | Size guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shelf above front-loaders | Detergent, stain remover, pegs | 30–50 cm above machines, 25–30 cm deep |
| Shelf above a top-loader | Backups, less-used items | Above the open lid line |
| Narrow rolling cart in a side gap | Daily supplies, dryer balls | 12–20 cm wide carts are common |
| Over-door rack | Sprays, brushes, lint roller | Check door thickness and closing |
| Wall-mounted rail with hooks | Ironing board, mesh bags, brushes | Into studs or proper anchors |

Keep the products you use every wash at the front and at shoulder height. Decant powder into a lidded container with a scoop if the box is awkward — but keep the label or write the dosage on the lid.

Laundry pods and concentrated liquids are dangerous for children and pets. Store them high or in a cabinet with a child lock, never on a low open shelf.

**Gap-cart example:** the gap beside the washer is 22 cm. Subtract 2 cm for the skirting board and 2 cm for the hose bulge, leaving 18 cm. A cart listed at 18 cm wide will be tight; a 15 cm cart will actually roll in and out.

## Step 4: Sort Only as Much as You Need

Three-section sorters look organized, but they take up 60–80 cm of floor. Ask whether you really run separate loads.

- **Most households:** one divided hamper (lights/darks) is enough.
- **Laundry closet:** skip hampers there. Keep dirty laundry in bedrooms and carry it over in one collapsible basket.
- **Families:** one hamper per bedroom, and each person brings theirs on wash day.

Put any sorter where clothes come off — usually the bedroom or bathroom — not necessarily beside the machine.

## Step 5: Solve Drying Before Storage

Drying is where most small laundry spaces fail.

- **Wall-mounted fold-down rack** — holds roughly a load of shirts and folds flat to 10 cm. Needs solid wall fixing.
- **Ceiling pulley airer** — uses empty height; great above a bath or in a utility room with a high ceiling.
- **Tension rod or hanging rail** — hang shirts on hangers straight from the machine.
- **Freestanding wing rack** — cheapest, but takes floor space. Store it behind a door.

Damp laundry indoors adds a lot of moisture to the air. Open a window, run an extractor fan or use a dehumidifier to avoid condensation and mold on cold walls.

## Step 6: Add a Folding Surface That's Safe

Over front-loaders, a worktop on wall battens or legs gives you a folding bench and a place for baskets. It must not rest on the machines — vibration will shift it. Leave the gap above the appliances that the manual specifies, often 2–3 cm.

For a top-loader or a tight closet, use a nearby table, the top of a dryer, or a fold-down wall-mounted table. Try folding one load where the surface would go before you build anything.

## Step 7: Make Putting Away the Easy Step

Give each person a basket that clean clothes go straight into, and have them carry it to their room. Keep spare hangers on a rail or hook in the laundry area so shirts can be hung while they're still warm. The less handling between machine and wardrobe, the less likely a pile forms.

## Mistakes to Avoid

- **Blocking the top-loader lid** with a shelf or cabinet.
- **Resting a worktop on the machines.** Support it independently.
- **Low, open storage for pods and detergent** in a home with children or pets.
- **Three hampers in a closet** that only fits one basket.
- **Ignoring airflow.** Damp clothes without ventilation lead to mold.

## Review After a Week

If dirty clothes still pile up, move the hamper closer to where clothes come off. If supplies are still out on top of the machine, the shelf is too high or too far. If clean clothes sit unfolded, the problem is the surface or the baskets, not storage.
