The reason a blank wall is hard is not that there is nothing to put on it. It is that most of the things people try are too small. Get the size right and almost any of the options below will work; get it wrong and a genuinely good print will still look lost.

So before the list: whatever goes up should be roughly **two-thirds the width of the furniture beneath it**, or about half the wall where there is no furniture. One big thing beats five small things nearly every time. [How high to hang art](/post/how-high-to-hang-art/) covers the heights and the spacing once you have chosen.

## Things that go on walls

**One oversized piece.** The simplest answer and usually the best. A single large canvas or framed print sized to the furniture settles a wall instantly. If the budget is the obstacle, major museums release high-resolution images of out-of-copyright works for free — print one large and frame it simply.

**A pair.** Two matched frames side by side, hung with the same 5–8 cm gap you would use in a gallery wall. Reads as calm and deliberate, and it is far easier to get right than a nine-frame arrangement.

**A gallery wall.** More work than it looks, because the whole group has to behave as one rectangle. Lay it on the floor first, photograph it, then cut paper templates. Worth it over a sofa or up a staircase; rarely worth it on a small wall.

**A textile.** A quilt, a kilim, a woven hanging, a length of block-printed cotton on a dowel. Soft, warm, absorbs sound, and a large one costs less than the equivalent area of framed art. It is also the safest thing to hang above a bed.

**A mirror.** Does double duty, especially in a dark room — a mirror on the wall beside a window spreads daylight into the room. [How to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) covers the placement properly.

**A shelf, or three.** A wall can hold storage instead of decoration. A floating shelf with books, a plant and one object does more for a room than a mediocre print. Measure and fix it properly first — the [floating shelf checklist](/post/diy-floating-shelves/) goes through wall types and loads.

**Lighting.** A wall light, a swing-arm lamp beside a bed, a picture light over a print. Light on a wall changes a room in the evening in a way that nothing hanging flat against it can.

**Plants.** A trailing plant on a high shelf or a wall-mounted pot. Check the light the wall actually gets before committing to anything that needs sun.

**Hats, baskets, plates.** Objects you own, grouped. This goes wrong when the group is too sparse — it needs enough pieces, close enough together, to read as one shape.

**A leaning piece.** A large framed print leaning on a sideboard or a mantel rather than hung. Renter-friendly, no holes, and easy to change your mind about.

**A pinboard or a picture ledge.** For a room that changes — a child's room, a home office, a studio. A ledge takes frames you swap without re-drilling.

**Nothing.** A real option, and an underrated one.

## Above the bed, specifically

This is the wall people ask about most, and it has its own constraints because you sleep under it.

**Weight and fixings matter more here than anywhere else in the house.** Whatever goes up should be fixed into a stud or into masonry with an anchor rated comfortably above its weight — not a picture pin, not an adhesive strip. If you cannot find a stud and you are not confident about the fixing, change the plan rather than hoping.

Given that, the ranking for most bedrooms:

1. **A textile.** No glass, light, forgiving. The obvious first choice.
2. **A single wide piece, lightly framed.** Choose a frame without glass, or with acrylic rather than glass, if you can.
3. **A pair or a row of three.** Keeps each individual piece light.
4. **A mirror.** Possible with proper mounting and a safety-backed mirror; plenty of people still would rather not.
5. **A heavy carved anything.** No.

The gap rule applies: **15–25 cm between the top of the headboard and the bottom of the piece**, and about two-thirds the width of the bed. A 150 cm double wants roughly 100 cm of art.

Where the room is small, consider doing nothing above the bed and putting the effort into the wall you see when you walk in. [Cozy small bedroom ideas](/post/cozy-small-bedroom-ideas/) takes that further.

## Two easy things to make

**A fabric panel.** Stretch a metre of good fabric over a cheap canvas or a timber frame and staple it at the back. Large, soft, costs what the fabric costs, and you can change it.

**Pressed botanicals.** Press leaves or grasses flat for a fortnight between books, then mount them on plain card in simple frames. A row of three or five, evenly spaced, reads as a considered set. Free, and seasonal.

## Where it tends to go wrong

- **Too small.** The one failure that matters. Two-thirds of the furniture width.
- **Too high.** Centre at about 145 cm, or 15–25 cm above the furniture.
- **Filling every wall.** Leave somewhere for the eye to rest.
- **Adhesive strips on a textured or recently painted wall.** They lift the paint.
- **Heavy things over a bed on the wrong fixing.** Find the stud or change the plan.
- **Waiting for the perfect piece.** A wall with something adequate on it looks better than a blank one.

If the wall is the last thing bothering you, it is probably not the only thing — [why your room looks cheap](/post/why-does-my-room-look-cheap/) runs through the rest in the order worth fixing them.
