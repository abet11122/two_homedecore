A narrow hall is one of the few spaces where the decorating decisions are genuinely constrained by arithmetic. Start with the route, and whatever depth is left over is what you have to work with.

## Measure the route first

A comfortable walking route is **75–90 cm**. People carry shopping, push past each other, and come through with a bag on one shoulder.

Measure the hall's total width, then subtract 75 cm. What remains is your depth budget:

| Total hall width | Depth available | What fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 100 cm | Almost nothing | Wall-mounted only: hooks, a shallow shelf, art |
| 100–120 cm | 20–25 cm | A slim console or a narrow wall shelf |
| 120–140 cm | 30–40 cm | A tilting shoe cabinet, or a slim bench |
| Over 140 cm | 40 cm+ | A proper bench with storage underneath |

Measure with **every door open**, including the front door and any internal doors that swing into the hall. A hall that is 110 cm wide with the doors shut can be 60 cm wide at the moment someone walks in.

## Go to the walls

In most narrow halls the answer is that nothing stands on the floor at all.

**Hooks.** The highest-value thing in a narrow hall. 165–175 cm for adults, 100–120 cm for children, 15–20 cm apart. A row of good hooks does more than a console ever will.

**A shelf of 20–25 cm.** Deep enough for keys, post and a small lamp, shallow enough that nobody catches a hip on it. Mount it at around 85–95 cm — console height — so it reads as furniture rather than as a shelf.

**Framed art, run along the length.** This is the move that changes how a hall feels. A row of frames at a consistent centre height, evenly spaced, pulls the eye down the corridor and makes the length an asset. [How high to hang art](/post/how-high-to-hang-art/) has the heights and spacing.

What does not work is a scattering of small objects at varying heights. It chops the wall into fragments and the hall reads as busier and narrower.

**A mirror on a long wall**, not at the far end. On a side wall it widens the hall by reflecting the opposite wall. At the end it mostly reflects the corridor back at you, which emphasises the tunnel.

## Light it along its length

One fitting in the middle of the ceiling is what makes a hall feel like a service corridor. It puts a bright patch in the centre and leaves both ends in shadow, which shortens the hall and darkens the doorways.

Better:

- **Several small fittings** spaced down the length, so the light is even end to end.
- **Wall lights on one side**, which light the opposite wall and make the hall feel wider.
- **A lamp on the shelf**, if there is a socket. A pool of warm light at waist height does a lot in a space that otherwise has none.

Bulbs: **2700K**, and **CRI 90 or above**. A hall is usually full of flat painted surfaces, and poor-quality light makes them look grey. [How to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) covers this in more detail — much of it applies here, since most halls have no window at all.

## The floor

**A runner**, with 10–15 cm of floor showing on each side. The strip of floor each side is what stops it reading as badly fitted carpet. Choose a flat weave — a thick pile catches doors, and most halls have 10–15 mm of clearance under an internal door.

**Keep it clear.** The single most effective decorating decision in a narrow hall is that nothing lives on the floor. One pair of shoes per person, on a tray. Everything else on the wall or in another room.

## Colour

Both directions work, and the choice is about what the hall is for.

**Light** opens it up and suits a hall with no window, provided the lighting is sorted. Keep the contrast low — walls, woodwork and doors in close tones, so there are fewer edges chopping up a small space.

**Dark** is a legitimate and often better choice. A hall is somewhere you pass through, so it can afford to be a moment of depth, and it makes the rooms leading off it feel brighter by comparison.

A useful trick either way: painting the **end wall** a deeper tone pulls it visually closer, which stops a long hall reading as a tunnel.

## What tends to go wrong

- **Furniture before measuring.** 75–90 cm of route, then whatever is left.
- **Measuring with the doors shut.** Open them all first.
- **One ceiling light in the middle.** Even light along the length.
- **A mirror at the far end.** Put it on a side wall.
- **A thick runner.** Check the clearance under every door.
- **Small objects at scattered heights.** One run, one height, even spacing.
- **Anything on the floor.** The fastest way to make a narrow hall feel narrower.
