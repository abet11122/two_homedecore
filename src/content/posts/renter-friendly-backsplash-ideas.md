Two things decide this, and neither is how it looks: **what the wall is**, and **how close it gets to the hob**. Get those right and most of the options below work. Get them wrong and you are either repainting a wall when you leave or watching vinyl curl off above a gas ring.

## First: check the wall and the lease

**The wall.** Glazed tile and gloss paint are non-porous and generally release adhesive cleanly. Matt emulsion on plaster is the risky one — the paint film is often weaker than the adhesive bond, so the paint comes away with the product. Freshly painted walls are worse still; paint takes weeks to cure fully.

**The lease.** Wording on adhesives varies enormously. Some leases prohibit anything adhesive outright. Get agreement in writing before covering an area.

**The test.** Whatever you choose, stick a piece somewhere hidden — inside a cupboard door, low down behind the bin — and **leave it at least a week**. Then peel it slowly and look at the back of it. If paint comes away on a one-week test, it will be far worse after a year.

## The hob rule

**Nothing adhesive goes directly behind a hob unless the product is explicitly rated for it**, and most are not.

Vinyl softens, adhesives release, and some materials are outright flammable. This is the single most important line in this article, and it is routinely ignored in photographs of rental kitchens online.

Behind a hob, use a **freestanding metal or toughened glass splash guard** instead. They stand on the worktop, cost very little, wipe clean, and go in a box when you move.

## The options

**A freestanding panel.** A sheet of acrylic, aluminium or toughened glass cut to size and resting against the wall behind the worktop, held by the worktop itself or a bead of removable sealant along the bottom. Damages nothing, comes with you, and handles heat better than any adhesive option. The least exciting answer and usually the best one.

**Tile stickers over existing tiles.** If the kitchen already has tiles you dislike, individual stickers sized to the tiles are lower risk than film on paint, because glazed tile releases cleanly. They sit within the grout lines so the result reads as retiling rather than as a covering.

**Peel-and-stick tile sheets.** Vinyl or gel sheets with a tile pattern, often with some depth to them. Good coverage and quick. Best on tile or gloss paint, risky on matt emulsion, and genuinely not suitable near heat. Clean and dry the wall thoroughly first — grease is the main cause of early lifting.

**Thin wall panels.** PVC or composite sheets, usually large format. They cover a big area fast and handle steam well. Most need some fixing, so this is a permission conversation rather than a weekend decision.

**Contact paper.** The cheapest. Also the thinnest, the least heat-tolerant and the most likely to telegraph every imperfection in the wall beneath. Fine for a small area away from the cooking zone; not a worktop-length solution.

**Paint.** Where the lease allows redecoration, a few coats of a hard-wearing paint in a wipeable finish behind the worktop is cheap and fully reversible by repainting. Not suitable immediately behind a hob.

**A shelf with a lip instead.** Sidestepping the question: a narrow shelf with a rail at worktop-plus-50 cm takes the visual interest and the wall stays plain. Covered in [open shelving in the kitchen](/post/kitchen-open-shelving-styling/).

## Comparing them

| Option | Damage risk | Near a hob | Comes with you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Freestanding panel | None | Yes, if metal or glass | Yes |
| Tile stickers on tile | Low | No | No |
| Peel-and-stick sheets | Medium to high on paint | No | No |
| Wall panels | Needs permission | Check the product | No |
| Contact paper | Medium | No | No |
| Paint | Low, repaintable | No | No |

## Fitting it so it lasts

- **Degrease the wall** and let it dry completely. Grease behind a worktop is invisible and ruins adhesion.
- **Work from a level line**, not from the worktop — worktops are rarely level, and a backsplash following one drifts visibly.
- **Cut around sockets carefully**, and turn off the power at the consumer unit before going anywhere near a socket faceplate. If a faceplate needs removing, that is an electrician's job in a rental.
- **Seal the bottom edge** with a removable sealant where it meets the worktop, so water does not get behind it.
- **Keep offcuts.** You will want them for repairs, and for patching when you remove it.

## Taking it off again

Warm the surface with a hairdryer on a low setting to soften the adhesive. Peel **slowly, back on itself at a shallow angle**, rather than pulling outwards from the wall — the shallow angle is what leaves the paint behind instead of taking it.

Residue comes off with an adhesive remover suited to the surface, tested somewhere hidden first. Work in small sections.

The longer it has been up, the harder it will be. That is worth factoring in at the point of sticking it on, not at the point of moving out.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Anything adhesive behind the hob.** Use a freestanding guard.
- **Skipping the hidden test patch.** A week minimum.
- **Sticking to fresh matt emulsion.** The highest-risk surface there is.
- **Not degreasing first.** The usual reason edges lift.
- **Levelling off the worktop.** Use a spirit level.
- **Peeling outwards when removing.** Shallow angle, warmed, slow.
