Everything in a small bathroom needs a home within reach of where it's used, and nothing can block the door, the toilet or the cabinet hinges. The route to that is unglamorous: declutter first, measure each storage zone, and only then buy organizers sized to those measurements.

A typical small bathroom — around 1.5 × 2 m (5 × 7 ft) — has five places storage can go: under the sink, the counter and the wall above the toilet, the back of the door, the shower, and the floor gaps beside the fixtures. Most clutter comes from one of them being unused while the counter takes the overflow. This page is the plan for all five; the two biggest zones have their own detailed guides, linked where they come up.

## Start here: declutter, then measure

**Declutter before buying anything.** Empty the drawers, vanity and shelves onto a towel on the floor. Bin anything expired, near-empty duplicates, and products nobody has touched in six months. Move out what belongs elsewhere: spare bedding, medicines labeled "store below 25°C", bulk cleaning refills. What's left tells you how much storage you actually need. Skipping this step is the most common mistake — people buy storage for things they don't use, and the organizers become clutter too.

**Then measure every zone** and write the numbers down:

| Zone | What to measure |
| --- | --- |
| Under the sink | Width, depth and height on **each side** of the drain trap, plus the cabinet lip and hinges |
| Above the toilet | Clear width between fittings; height from the cistern lid to the ceiling |
| Door | Thickness (most interior doors are 35–45 mm) and the gap above it for over-door hooks |
| Floor gaps | Width beside the toilet or vanity, at floor level and at counter height |
| Clearances | At least **53 cm** (21 in) of clear floor in front of the toilet and sink, 76 cm (30 in) if you can |

Those clearances follow common bathroom planning guidelines; local building rules apply if you're renovating.

## Zone 1: Under the sink

The trap usually splits the cabinet into a left and a right zone, so organize each side separately rather than buying one wide box.

**Example:** a vanity has 48 cm of internal width, but the trap and hinges leave two 18 cm side zones. A 45 cm drawer unit chosen from the product listing would not fit. Two 15 cm bins would.

Make a paper template of any organizer's footprint and lay it in the cabinet to check that the doors close and the bin lifts out past the hinges. Never block the shutoff valves, and if you find damp or swollen board, fix the leak before filling the cabinet.

Inside, group by broad category — hair, skin, dental, first aid, cleaning, backups — so everyone in the house can put things back without thinking. Clear bins show what's running low; opaque baskets look calmer on open shelves. Cleaning products go in a lockable cabinet or on a high shelf, out of children's reach.

## Zone 2: The counter and the wall above the toilet

**Keep only daily essentials on the counter**: soap, toothbrushes, one skincare product. A clear counter makes a small bathroom feel bigger and takes seconds to wipe. What stays out goes on one moisture-resistant tray, about 20 × 30 cm on most small vanities, so five loose bottles lift off in one move when you clean.

**Move toothbrushes onto the wall** if the vanity is tiny: a wall-mounted holder or a narrow mirrored cabinet frees surprising space. Keep them at least 1 m from the toilet where you can.

**Use the wall above the toilet**, often the largest unused surface in the room. Keep shelves **15–20 cm deep** and mount the lowest one **60–75 cm above the cistern** so the lid lifts and nobody hits their head standing up. Store only light, unbreakable things there: spare rolls, hand towels, a small plant. Renters can use a freestanding over-toilet frame, which still needs its anti-tip strap. Shelves versus cabinets versus frames, and how to fix each one safely, are in the [over-toilet storage guide](/post/bathroom-over-toilet-storage-ideas/).

**Hooks before furniture.** Two or three hooks take no floor space and hold robes, hand towels or a hanging basket. In a rental, check that adhesive hooks are rated for humid rooms and press them for the full time on the pack.

## Zone 3: The door, the shower and the floor gaps

**The back of the door.** An over-door rack or slim hanging organizer holds towels, hair tools or toiletries. Check that the hooks suit the door's thickness and that the loaded organizer doesn't stop the door closing. Felt pads on the back stop rattling.

**The shower.** A tension-pole corner caddy or a properly fixed shelf lifts bottles off the shower floor. Keep it clear of the controls and any grab bar, and take it down every few weeks to clean behind it. Suction products can let go without warning, so nothing glass or heavy goes on one. A recessed niche is a renovation, not an add-on: plan it with someone qualified.

**The floor gaps.** A rolling cart 15–20 cm wide often fits beside a toilet or vanity. Daily items on top, towels and backups below. Measure the gap at floor level and at counter height, since skirting boards and countertop overhangs both steal width.

**Towels.** Roll hand towels and washcloths into a basket, where you can see how many are clean. Fold full-size bath towels in thirds, which usually takes less shelf space than rolling.

**Backups.** Keep only the product in use in the bathroom, and one backup bin somewhere else, such as a hall cupboard. Don't buy another backup until the last one is opened. Steam and heat shorten many products' shelf life, so this protects them too.

## A budget plan in three levels

| Level | What to buy | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter | Drawer dividers, 2 bins, a tray, 3 adhesive hooks | $25–$50 |
| Mid | Add a turntable, under-sink pull-out baskets, an over-door rack | $70–$130 |
| Full | Add over-toilet shelves or a frame, a tension shower caddy, a slim cart | $150–$250 |

These are rough ranges from mainstream homeware stores and vary by region. Reused boxes and jars cost nothing and are a good way to test a layout before buying fitted organizers.

## Five common mistakes

- **Buying by overall cabinet size.** Pipes, hinges and the front lip shrink usable space. Measure each zone separately.
- **Deep shelves above the toilet.** Anything over about 20 cm becomes a head hazard and blocks the cistern lid.
- **Heavy or breakable things overhead.** Glass jars and full bottles on a high shelf can fall into the toilet or onto someone.
- **Too many tiny categories.** A system that needs a label for every item won't survive a busy week.
- **Ignoring humidity.** Cardboard, untreated wood and some adhesives fail in a steamy room. Choose washable, moisture-resistant materials.

## A one-week test

After organizing, live with it for a week and notice what still ends up on the counter every day. That item needs a reachable home closer to where it's used, or there are simply too many of it in the room. Fix that one spot first. More containers only help when they make putting things away as easy as leaving them out.
