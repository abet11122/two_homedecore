Everything in a small bathroom needs a home within reach of where it's used, and nothing can block the door, the toilet or the cabinet hinges. The fastest route to that is unglamorous: declutter first, measure each storage zone, and only then buy organizers sized to those measurements.

A typical small bathroom — around 1.5 × 2 m (5 × 7 ft) — has three hard-working zones: the vanity and under-sink cabinet, the wall above the toilet, and the back of the door. Most clutter problems come from one of them being underused while the counter takes the overflow. The 17 ideas below are grouped by zone so you can fix the worst one first.

## Start Here: Declutter and Measure

### 1. Declutter Before Buying Organizers

Empty the drawers, vanity and open shelves onto a towel on the floor. Bin anything expired, nearly empty duplicates, and products nobody has touched in six months. Move items that belong elsewhere — spare bedding, medicines with "store below 25°C" labels, bulk cleaning refills — out of the room.

Skipping this step is the most common mistake: people buy storage for things they don't need, and then the organizers become clutter too. What's left after decluttering tells you how much storage you actually need.

### 2. Map Every Storage Zone With a Tape Measure

Write down these measurements before shopping:

- **Vanity interior:** width, depth and height on each side of the plumbing. A standard vanity is about 53 cm (21 in) deep inside, but the drain trap usually takes the middle third.
- **Wall above the toilet:** clear width between fittings, and height from the cistern lid to the ceiling.
- **Door:** thickness (most interior doors are 35–45 mm), and the gap above the door for over-door hooks.
- **Floor gaps:** the space beside the toilet or vanity, measured at floor level and at hip height.
- **Clearances:** keep at least 53 cm (21 in) of clear floor in front of the toilet and sink — 76 cm (30 in) is more comfortable — and make sure the door still opens fully.

These clearances follow common bathroom planning guidelines; local building rules apply if you're renovating.

## Zone 1: The Vanity and Under-Sink Cabinet

### 3. Organize Around the Pipes, Not Over Them

Under-sink space is usually split into a left and a right zone by the trap. Choose organizers for each side rather than one wide box. U-shaped drawers, two narrow pull-out baskets, or a pair of stackable bins all work. Make a paper template of the organizer's footprint and lay it inside the cabinet to check that the doors close and the bin lifts out without hitting the hinges.

Never block the shutoff valves. If you find damp, swollen board or a drip, fix it before filling the cabinet.

**Example:** a vanity has 48 cm of clear internal width, but the trap and hinges leave two 18 cm side zones. A 45 cm drawer unit bought from the product listing would not fit. Two 15 cm-wide bins would.

### 4. Use Clear or Labeled Bins by Category

Group items into broad categories: hair, skin, dental, first aid, cleaning and backups. Clear bins show what's running low; opaque baskets look calmer on open shelves. Keep categories broad enough that everyone in the house can put things back without thinking.

Store cleaning products in a lockable cabinet or on a high shelf, out of children's sight and reach.

### 5. Add Two Short Stackable Drawers

Small stacking drawers create a second level inside a tall cabinet for cotton pads, razors, makeup and travel sizes. Two short levels are easier to use than one tall tower, where the top drawer ends up too high to pull out.

### 6. Fit Drawer Dividers

Dividers stop toothbrush heads, hair ties and makeup from becoming one mixed layer. Measure the drawer's usable height under the countertop lip — it's often 2–3 cm less than the drawer box. Adjustable bamboo dividers or cut-down shoeboxes both work.

### 7. Add a Turntable for Bottles

A turntable (lazy Susan) brings bottles at the back of a deep cabinet to the front with one turn. A 25–28 cm diameter fits most vanity sides. Choose one with a raised rim so bottles don't topple, and keep it for similar-height items like hair products or lotions.

## Zone 2: Counter and Walls

### 8. Keep Only Daily Essentials on the Counter

Limit the counter to what's used every day: soap, toothbrushes, one skincare product. Everything else goes into closed storage. A visible counter makes a small bathroom feel bigger and takes seconds to wipe.

### 9. Corral Small Items on One Tray

A tray turns five loose bottles into one group that lifts off in a single move when you clean. Pick a moisture-resistant material: sealed wood, stone, metal or washable plastic. A 20 × 30 cm tray suits most small vanities.

### 10. Move Toothbrushes Onto the Wall

On a very small vanity, a wall-mounted toothbrush holder or a narrow mirrored cabinet frees up surprising space. Keep toothbrushes at least 1 m from the toilet where you can, and pick a holder that goes in the dishwasher or comes apart for cleaning.

### 11. Put Shelves Above the Toilet

The wall above the toilet is often the largest unused surface in the room. Keep shelves 15–20 cm deep and mount the lowest one high enough that you can lift the cistern lid and stand up without ducking — usually 60–75 cm above the cistern. Use these shelves for light items: spare toilet rolls, hand towels, a small plant.

Renters can use a freestanding over-toilet frame instead. Follow the maker's anti-tip instructions — "freestanding" doesn't mean it can't fall. For a deeper look at shelf, cabinet and frame options, see the [over-toilet storage guide](/post/bathroom-over-toilet-storage-ideas/).

### 12. Use Hooks Instead of More Furniture

Two or three hooks take no floor space and hold robes, hand towels or a hanging basket. Place towel hooks where fabric can dry without touching the toilet or floor. Adhesive hooks suit rentals; check the weight rating and whether the adhesive is rated for humid rooms, and press for the full time listed on the pack.

## Zone 3: Door, Shower and Floor Gaps

### 13. Use the Back of the Door

An over-door rack or slim hanging organizer holds towels, hair tools or toiletries without using wall or floor. Check that the hooks suit your door's thickness and that the loaded organizer doesn't stop the door from closing or scrape the frame. Felt pads on the back stop rattling.

### 14. Choose Shower Storage Rated for Wet Conditions

A tension-pole corner caddy or a properly mounted shelf lifts bottles off the shower floor. Check that it doesn't block the controls or any grab bar, and take it down every few weeks to clean behind it. Suction products can let go without warning, so never put glass or heavy bottles on one.

A recessed niche is a renovation, not an add-on: wall cavities vary and a niche in a wet wall needs proper waterproofing. Plan it with someone qualified.

### 15. Slot a Rolling Cart Into a Floor Gap

A cart 15–20 cm wide can fit beside a toilet or vanity when there's no built-in storage. Top shelf for daily items, lower shelves for towels and backups. Measure the gap at floor level and at the height of the vanity top — baseboards and countertop overhangs steal width.

### 16. Roll Small Towels, Fold Big Ones

Rolled hand towels and washcloths fit neatly in baskets and show at a glance how many are clean. Full-size bath towels usually take less space folded in thirds, so match the method to the shelf.

### 17. Store Backups Outside the Bathroom

Keep only the product in use in the shower and one backup bin elsewhere — a hall cupboard or the top of a wardrobe. A simple rule keeps it under control: don't buy another backup until the last one is opened. Steam and heat also shorten the shelf life of many products, so this protects them too.

## A Budget Plan in Three Levels

| Level | What to buy | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter | Drawer dividers, 2 bins, a tray, 3 adhesive hooks | $25–$50 |
| Mid | Add a turntable, under-sink pull-out baskets, an over-door rack | $70–$130 |
| Full | Add an over-toilet unit or shelves, a tension shower caddy, a slim cart | $150–$250 |

Prices are rough ranges from mainstream homeware stores and vary by region. Reused boxes and jars cost nothing and are a good way to test a layout before buying fitted organizers.

## Five Common Mistakes
- **Buying by overall cabinet size.** Pipes, hinges and the front lip shrink usable space. Measure each zone separately.
- **Deep shelves above the toilet.** Anything over about 20 cm becomes a head hazard and blocks the cistern lid.
- **Heavy or breakable items overhead.** Glass jars and full bottles on a high shelf can fall into the toilet or onto someone.
- **Too many tiny categories.** A system that needs a label for every item won't survive a busy week.
- **Ignoring humidity.** Cardboard, untreated wood and some adhesives fail in a steamy room. Choose washable, moisture-resistant materials.

## A One-Week Test

After you organize, live with it for a week. Notice what still ends up on the counter every day. That item needs a reachable home closer to where it's used, or there are simply too many of it in the room. Adjust that one spot first. More containers only help when they make putting things away as easy as leaving them out.
