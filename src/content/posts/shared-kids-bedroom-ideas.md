Two children in one room is mostly a territory problem wearing a storage problem's clothes. Children who feel they have somewhere that is *theirs* share a room reasonably well. Children sharing one undivided space with one shared toy box argue about everything, and no amount of clever furniture fixes it.

So: decide the beds, then divide the territory, then sort the storage — in that order.

## The bed arrangements

**Bunk beds.** The most floor saved by a long way, which in a small room is the whole argument. Expect to lose the use of the wall above the lower bunk, and accept that making the top bed is genuinely annoying.

On safety: follow the specific product's instructions rather than general advice. Manufacturers set their own limits, and **many specify a minimum age of six for the top bunk**. Guard rails on all open sides, a fixed ladder, and the assembly done to the letter — this is not the furniture to improvise with, and a second-hand frame without its instructions and fittings is not worth the saving.

**An L-shape in the corner.** Two single beds meeting at right angles, often with storage in the corner where they join. Keeps both children at ground level, which suits younger ones or a room with a low or sloping ceiling. Uses more floor than bunks but far less than two parallel beds.

**Parallel beds with a gap.** The most conventional, and the best for two children who need obvious separation. Needs the most floor: two single beds plus a usable gap is about 2.6 m of width at minimum.

**Head-to-head along one wall.** Beds end to end down a long wall, with a shared surface between the headboards. Good in a long narrow room, which is otherwise a difficult shape — [how to arrange furniture in a small room](/post/how-to-arrange-furniture-in-a-small-room/) covers that shape generally.

**A trundle.** One bed visible, the second pulling out from underneath. Best where the sharing is part-time — a child who stays at weekends, or siblings who only share sometimes.

Whichever you pick, apply the normal clearances: **60 cm beside a bed** a child gets in and out of, **90 cm in front of a wardrobe**. [Where to put the bed in a small room](/post/where-to-put-bed-in-small-room/) has the full set.

## Give each child a territory

This matters more than the furniture and costs almost nothing.

**One wall each.** Each child decides what goes on their wall — posters, their own drawings, a pinboard. The point is not the decoration; it is that the decision is theirs. One child's wall looking nothing like the other's is a success, not a problem.

**Their own light.** A clip-on or wall-mounted reading light each. This solves the bedtime problem below and reinforces that each half of the room belongs to someone.

**Their own storage.** Covered below, and the single biggest reducer of arguments.

**Something that marks the boundary.** A rug each, a different wall colour on each half, a bookcase placed end-on between the beds, or a curtain on a ceiling track. A curtain between bunks is worth a lot once children reach the age of wanting privacy.

What you do *not* need is matching everything. A room where both children have identical bedding, identical shelves and identical lamps looks tidy to an adult and feels to a child like neither half is theirs.

## Storage: divide it

The rule is simple. **Shared storage causes arguments; divided storage does not.**

- **Clothes:** separate drawers, or clearly separate halves of one chest. Label them while children are young.
- **Toys:** one labelled box each for the things that are theirs, plus genuinely shared boxes for the things that actually are shared — building blocks, board games.
- **School things:** a tray, shelf or hook each, at that child's height.
- **Books:** a shelf each at a reachable height.

Height matters more than quantity for younger children. Storage a four-year-old cannot reach is storage an adult operates on their behalf. [Small boys bedroom ideas](/post/small-boys-bedroom-ideas/) covers heights by age; the same applies regardless of who is in the room.

Then use the places a shared room has and a single room does not: **under both beds** (shallow boxes on wheels), **the wall above the lower bunk's foot end**, and **the back of the door**.

## Different ages, different bedtimes

The hardest practical problem in a shared room, and the one most often attacked with rules when it needs equipment.

- **Individual lights** with a directed beam, so one child can read while the other sleeps. A clip-on light with an opaque shade beats a bedside lamp here.
- **A curtain or canopy** on the older child's bunk.
- **Stagger the routine rather than the room:** the younger child goes down first, the older one comes in later having already brushed their teeth elsewhere.
- **A white-noise source** helps a lighter sleeper more than silence does.

## As they get older

The thing that changes is not the sharing but the privacy — somewhere to get changed, and somewhere to keep things that are not the sibling's business.

A curtain between beds, a folding screen, or taking turns using the room covers most of it. A lockable drawer or a box with a lid matters more to a ten-year-old than another shelf does.

## What tends to go wrong

- **Shared toy storage.** Divide it and most arguments stop.
- **A bunk bought second-hand without its instructions.** Check the manufacturer's guidance and age limits.
- **Matching everything.** Each child needs their half to look like theirs.
- **Storage above a small child's reach.** Height by age, not by tidiness.
- **One light for the room.** Individual lights solve bedtimes.
- **Leaving no floor.** Children need somewhere to play; pick the bed arrangement that protects it.
