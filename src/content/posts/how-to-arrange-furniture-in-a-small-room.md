Begin with the routes, not the furniture. Walk the room the way you normally do — door to window, door to sofa, sofa to kitchen — and mark those lines. Everything else gets arranged around them, because a layout that looks good on paper and blocks the path to the window will be undone within a fortnight.

## The measurements that decide it

Four numbers cover nearly every decision in a small room:

| Gap | Width | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main walkway | 75–90 cm (30–36 in) | Two people pass, or one person carries something |
| Secondary route | 60 cm minimum (24 in) | One person at a time, used occasionally |
| Sofa to coffee table | 40–45 cm (16–18 in) | Reach a cup without standing; still walk through |
| In front of a wardrobe or drawers | 90 cm (36 in) | Door or drawer fully open, plus you |

Below 60 cm on any route, people start turning sideways to get through, and that is exactly the sensation of a cramped room — regardless of the floor area.

## Find the focal point, then commit

Every room has one thing the seating should face: a window with a view, a fireplace, the television. Decide which it is and arrange around it.

The common failure is trying to serve two. A sofa angled hopefully between the window and the TV serves neither, and leaves awkward triangles of floor that nothing fits into.

If the room genuinely has two — a fireplace and a TV on different walls — pick the one you use more in the evening. In most houses the honest answer is the television, and there is no shame in arranging a living room around the thing you actually look at. ## Come off the walls

The instinct in a small room is to push every piece flat against a wall to keep the middle clear. It feels like it should create space. It produces a room that looks like a waiting area, with a useless void in the centre and no sense of enclosure anywhere.

**Pull the sofa 5–10 cm off the wall.** That is all. The shadow behind it reads as depth, the piece stops looking stuck on, and the room looks more deliberate. You lose almost no floor.

In a genuinely tiny room — a box bedroom, a 3 × 3 m sitting room — this one does not apply. There, use the walls and save every centimetre of floor.

## Make the furniture look lighter

Visual weight matters as much as footprint in a small room.

**Legs.** Furniture on visible legs lets the floor run underneath it, and a floor you can see reads as a bigger floor. A sofa with a skirt to the ground occupies the same area and looks like twice as much.

**Arms.** A slim-armed sofa gives you 15–20 cm more seat for the same overall width. In a small room that is a whole person.

**Height.** Keep most pieces low and let one thing be tall — a bookcase, a plant, a floor lamp — so the eye travels up somewhere.

**Fewer, larger.** One properly scaled sofa beats a loveseat plus two chairs. Three small pieces read as clutter; one large piece reads as calm.

**See-through.** A glass or open-frame coffee table disappears in a way a solid block does not.

## Long narrow rooms

The hardest shape, and the one where instinct is most wrong. Running the sofa and everything else down the long walls turns the room into a corridor with furniture in it.

Split it into two zones instead. Seating at one end, something else at the other — a desk, a small dining table, a reading chair. Then:

- **Float the sofa across the width**, so it cuts the room rather than lining it.
- **Give each zone a rug.** Two rugs do more to define two areas than any amount of furniture arrangement.
- **Put something with height at the far end**, so the eye stops there rather than running to the wall.

## Test it before you lift anything

Cut the footprint of each major piece out of newspaper and lay the shapes on the floor. Live with them for a day. Walk the routes, open the doors, pull a chair out.

It takes twenty minutes, and it is the difference between knowing a layout works and finding out after you have dragged a sofa across the room twice.

Check the door swings while you are at it — internal doors, wardrobe doors, the oven door if the room includes a kitchen.

## Layout mistakes in small rooms

- **Arranging furniture before mapping the routes.** The path wins; it always wins.
- **Everything flat against the walls.** Come off by 5–10 cm.
- **Serving two focal points.** Pick one.
- **Several small pieces instead of one right-sized one.** Fewer and larger.
- **A coffee table too close or too far.** 40–45 cm.
- **Skirted furniture in a small room.** Legs show the floor.
- **Running everything down a long room's long walls.** Float across it instead.
