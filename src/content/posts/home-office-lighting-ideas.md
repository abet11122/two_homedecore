Most home offices are lit by whatever fitting the room came with, usually in the middle of the ceiling and usually too cool. That gives you glare on the screen, a shadow over your own hands, and a face lit from directly above on every call. Three positions fix almost all of it.

## Put the window to one side

This is the decision that matters most, and it is free.

**Perpendicular to the desk.** Daylight crosses the surface from one side. No glare on the screen, no silhouette, and the light is even.

**Not behind the monitor.** Your eyes spend the day adjusting between a bright window and a darker screen, which is the main cause of the headache people blame on screen time.

**Not behind you.** It reflects straight off the screen, and on a call it blows out the camera and turns you into a dark shape.

In a small room you may only have one wall that takes a desk, and that is that. Where the window has to be behind the screen, a sheer blind diffuses it rather than blocking it. The desk placement rules in [small home office ideas](/post/small-home-office-ideas/) cover what else constrains the position.

## Light the desk, not the room

A ceiling fitting lights the floor and the top of your head. It does very little for the surface you are working on, and it is the reason so many desks feel gloomy in a room that is technically bright.

**A task lamp on the opposite side from your writing hand.** Left side for a right-hander. Put it on the same side and your hand shadows whatever you are writing.

**An adjustable arm beats a fixed one.** You want to move the light off the keyboard and onto paper and back.

**Aim it at the work, not at the screen.** A lamp pointing anywhere near the monitor produces reflections you will spend the day tilting your head around.

**Under-shelf lighting** if a shelf sits above the desk. A strip on the underside lights the surface without a lamp taking up space — useful on a desk measured in centimetres.

As a rough target, detailed desk work wants something in the region of 300–500 lux at the surface. Rather than buying a meter: if you lean in to read small print, or your eyes ache after an hour, add a task lamp before you reach for a brighter ceiling bulb.

## The two numbers on the box

**Colour temperature.** 4000K through the working day — neutral, closer to daylight, and it keeps the room feeling like a workspace. Then 2700K in the evening, so the room rejoins the rest of the house. A smart bulb that shifts between the two is one of the few genuinely useful applications of one.

**CRI.** Colour rendering index, 0–100. Below 80 and everything in the room looks slightly dead — paper goes grey, wood goes flat, skin looks ill on camera. **CRI 90 or above.** It costs a little more per bulb and it is the difference between a room that is lit and a room that looks good lit. The same point applies across the house; [how to make a dark room brighter](/post/how-to-make-a-dark-room-brighter/) goes into it further.

## Lighting for calls

Different problem, different answer. Working light comes from the side; camera light comes from the front.

- **Main light in front of you, at roughly eye level**, slightly off to one side. A lamp bounced off a pale wall is enough — it does not need to be a ring light.
- **Overhead light alone is the worst case.** It drops shadows into your eye sockets.
- **Nothing bright behind you.** Close the blind or turn the desk.
- **Match the temperatures.** A warm lamp on one side and a cool one on the other makes skin look patchy on camera.

Check what the camera actually sees rather than trusting the room. Open the camera app, look, and move one lamp.

## Reducing screen glare

- **Tilt the screen slightly down.** It stops ceiling lights landing on it.
- **Check for a reflection of the lamp** in the screen with the monitor switched off — the black surface shows every light source in the room.
- **Match the screen brightness to the room**, rather than running it at maximum against a dim background.
- **A matte screen** if you cannot control the reflections any other way.

## In a room doing two jobs

Where the office shares a bedroom or a guest room, the lighting has to switch roles at the end of the day. A desk lamp you can turn off independently, on a separate switch or plug, is what lets the room stop being an office — which matters more than it sounds for sleeping in. [Guest bedroom and office combo](/post/guest-bedroom-office-combo/) covers the rest of that changeover.

## Where it goes wrong

- **The window behind the screen.** Glare all day.
- **The window behind you.** Reflections, and a silhouette on calls.
- **Ceiling light only.** It lights the floor, not the desk.
- **The lamp on your writing hand's side.** Your own shadow.
- **Cool bulbs left on in the evening.** The room never stops feeling like work.
- **CRI under 80.** Everything looks grey, including you.
